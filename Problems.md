# Problems

What is wrong with or missing from the Constitution. Nothing here is decided: a proposed fix is a starting point. Remarks accumulate under each Problem as thinking develops. Delete a Problem once a Hearing shows the text handles it; Git keeps its history.

Article references use the current numbering.

## From the second hearings

P1, P2, P4, P6, P7 and P9 were confirmed at the third hearings and removed; P8, P20, P43 and P53 at the 5 November hearings. P5 was only partly confirmed; notes below. P3 waits for the redesign in P31.

### P3. Public things belong to no one

Graffiti on a public underpass came out unclear, because no one lives or works there, so no one possesses it.

> Revised direction: nearlaw owns little itself. Commons are held by the people who use them, organised in local commons bodies that set by-laws for their use, as in Elinor Ostrom's studies of self-governed commons. This overlaps with P31: commons bodies may be the small, geographic layer, with chosen communities as the non-geographic one.

**Discussion (4 November):** the public lift in the Luna pilot belongs here, with P40, not in a new Problem. Distinguish personal use, shared use and custody for others. A worker caring for a shared thing must not thereby acquire it personally, nor must an outside investor acquire a claim to land merely by financing it. Collective use must not depend on every user being present at once. Test a lift used by a residents' cooperative with and without damage by an outsider; separately test whether its members can exclude an existing user or enclose a previously open commons. These are proposed cases, not settled rules.

**Hearings (5 November):** graffiti on a public underpass used by hundreds daily still split: Haiku found no breach, since no one occupies or holds the walls; Luna could not say whether anyone holds them. A visitor smashing a residents' shared lift was mostly found a breach of II.2.6, treating the residents as holding it, though one run was unclear. Shared use seems to count as holding when users are identifiable, and not otherwise.

**Candidate, not applied:** add to Holding: "Things kept for shared use are held in common by their users. Keeping a thing for others gives no exclusive claim to it." For shared places that no one occupies, extend II.2.5 to protect "places kept for shared use from damage or being taken out of that use without their users' consent". This would protect common use without granting absentee land title or making an organisation a patient. Who counts as a user, how users consent together, and how use may change without enclosure remain open under P31/P40; the shared-place addition is not ready to adopt.

### P5. A long lapse period could restore absentee ownership (applied)

The lapse period is left to ordinary law, so a future parliament could set it at 50 years. Both arbiters also wondered whether an "owner" has some entitlement beyond possession.

> Revised direction: drop "unused" entirely, since any use test invites token gestures, like planting a few potatoes to show croft land is "in use". Under P4, occupancy is a fact: where someone lives or works. Land that is no one's home or workplace is unoccupied and open to settlement (see P31), and land value tax prices occupying more than one's share. Keep "No one holds land or things except by occupancy or holding." Open option: a person occupies one home at a time.

**Third hearings:** the empty-building case was still unclear in both hearings. Open edges: whether the forced door is a thing the absent owner still *holds*, since holding ends only when a thing is given away or discarded; whether occasional use, storage or intended return counts as occupancy; and whether an owner that is a company can occupy anything (P40).

**Hearings (5 November):** T23 now mostly passes; one run still asked whether the door is held. See P56 for the fixture probe, which shows that the doubt is real.

## Recurring gaps

These were flagged in almost every one of the 28 second-round rulings, and are now the main source of noise in hearings.

### P10. The boundary court doesn't exist yet (applied, without its make-up)

Article IV.2.5 leaves open who decides whether a matter is near or far. The working idea is a court chosen by lot, so neither government appoints it, applying one test: is there an outside party, or a crossing? Its size, term and powers are undecided.

**Options:**

1. **A panel chosen by lot.** Around seven citizens on short fixed terms, deciding only near-or-far questions, binding on both governments. No government appoints anyone, so neither can capture it. The cost is uneven expertise and possibly inconsistent rulings, eased by publishing reasons that later panels follow.
2. **A joint panel with a lot-chosen chair.** Each government appoints equal members, and a citizen chosen by lot holds the deciding vote. More expertise and buy-in, but the appointees become each side's advocates, and everything rests on one person.
3. **No court, just a default.** Every matter is nearlaw's unless farlaw shows an outside party or a crossing, and disputes go to the ordinary courts. Minimal and in keeping with farlaw's shorter list, but the ordinary courts are nearlaw's, so nearlaw would judge its own case.

**Suggested:** option 1, with option 3's default built in: anything not shown to be far is near.

**Remarks (2 November):** worried this could reach into every small matter. Refinement: the court hears only challenges. Each government acts on matters it believes are its own; only a person affected, an Advocate or the other government can challenge, within a time limit; and the court decides the category alone, never the merits.

### P11. No way to stop ongoing harm

Article II.2 now permits necessary and proportionate limits on specified privileges to defend against imminent death, injury or suffering. That includes confinement or moving someone from where they live, but only so far and for so long as necessary. It supplies no general power to detain for later prevention, punishment or investigation. Article II.2.4 states no condition on leaving Wroxia. Telling someone to stop is ordinary expression and needs no power, but how to stop repeated harm outside an imminent threat remains undecided. The coercive-control and graffiti rulings both noted the former absence of a preventive remedy.

A former draft proposed this condition on II.2.3: "Confinement, decided in a judicial case, of someone who carried on causing harm after being told to stop." It is a starting point, not part of the current constitution or a decision. See P46 on the judicial case and P48 on a shared home.

**Options:**

1. **Protective detainment.** Confinement allowed only while necessary to prevent further harm to others, in humane conditions, reviewed at short intervals by citizens chosen by lot, and ending as soon as it is no longer necessary. Never a set sentence. Keeps people safe, but "necessary" can stretch, which is why review matters.
2. **Keep-away orders first.** Someone who has caused harm can be ordered to stay away from particular people or places. Confinement is allowed only if they break the order and harm is again imminent. Far less loss of liberty, but it depends on the order being respected.
3. **No confinement at all.** Only defensive force in the moment, plus withholding public services (P41). The purest no-punishment answer, but it leaves repeated violence unchecked.

**Suggested:** option 2 with option 1 as the last resort. This would need an appropriate condition on Article II.2.3.

**Remarks (2 November):** sceptical of citizens chosen by lot deciding over someone's life: it risks personal liability and popularity contests. New idea to explore: each person nominates whom decisions about their life are deferred to, such as family, friends or coworkers, and that idea may apply elsewhere too.

**Drafting direction (4 November):** settle immediate defence separately from later prevention. Any defensive condition must expressly cover the privileges it limits, not assume that II.2.1 overrides II.2.3, II.2.5, II.2.6 or II.2.8. It must leave the absolute rights untouched. Restraint ends when no longer necessary against the imminent threat; it confers no power to punish, investigate or take unrelated possessions. An assertion of danger is not proof of it, and government still bears the burden under III.2.5.

For later prevention, the preferred starting point is a specific keep-away measure after an independent hearing, with confinement only as a last resort against serious further harm, prompt review and release when the justification ends. Thresholds, evidence, review intervals and who decides remain proposals, not delegated permission to detain (P38/P46). Removing the harmed person from a shared home must not be the default (P48).

**Proposed case pairs:** change only whether an attack is still imminent when a bystander restrains the attacker; whether that bystander also takes an unrelated wallet; and whether police continue restraint after the danger has ended. Separately compare the same imminent threat from someone able and unable to understand others' privileges (P57). T31/T32 test later removal and confinement without an imminent threat; T33–T38 and T41 test the new defence condition.

**Applied draft (4 November), not yet heard:** the shared Article II.2 condition replaces the narrower defence condition under II.2.1. It expressly covers II.2.1/II.2.2/II.2.3/II.2.5/II.2.6/II.2.8, leaves every absolute right untouched, and excludes protected expression as a ground for intervention. Articles III.2.8–III.2.9 add independent hearings and prompt review of involuntary confinement or removal, with the justification owed by whoever imposed it. T33–T38 and T41–T42 target the defence boundaries; T39–T40 target process. No new power of later preventive detention is adopted. T31/T32 remain tests of removal and confinement without an imminent threat.

**Hearings (5 November):** the defence condition holds. T31–T32 and T34–T39 agreed as intended, T40 after the prompt was clarified, and T42 too. T33 split only because one model judged Ilya's attack rather than Jo's restraint; both agreed the restraint was permitted. T41 agreed on breach but split on whether the confinement is forbiddable. What remains open is later prevention, not immediate defence.

### P12. Recovery of restitution (applied; remaining gaps)

Article III.2.3 now assigns recovery to a public recovery service of the government whose funds paid. Disputed liability or amounts go to a court under III.2.8. Taking held things to make restitution is allowed under II.2.6, but inability to pay and the limits of collection still need work.

**Options:**

1. **The Advocates collect.** The Advocate for the kind of right or privilege breached recovers restitution, taking held things to the value owed under the condition in II.2.6. Simple, but victims wait, and nothing can be recovered from someone with nothing.
2. **A restitution fund.** Nearlaw pays victims at once and recovers the cost from whoever caused the harm, over time. Victims are made whole quickly; the cost is public money and a risk of carelessness when someone else pays first.
3. **Debt with exclusion.** Unpaid restitution stays owed, and the debtor loses excludable public services until it's settled (P41). Gentle, but it can trap people who simply can't pay. Working it off can only ever be voluntary, since Article II.1.3 forbids compelled work.

**Earlier suggestion:** options 1 and 2 together: the fund pays at once, and the Advocate recovers from the person responsible.

**Decision (2 November):** accepted. Ready to draft.

**Third hearings:** the fund worked as intended in the wrecked-bicycle case. Unaddressed: how public funds recover from someone with nothing, given that work can't be compelled (II.1.3).

**Applied draft (4 November), not yet heard:** the paying government's recovery service replaces the earlier Advocate-collection proposal. Advocates remain free to represent patients, including a debtor subjected to unlawful recovery. Public payment is not postponed until collection succeeds.

**Suggested next steps, not adopted:** ordinary law could provide notice of the amount and its basis, voluntary instalments based on ability to pay, and court review before disputed collection. Decide what necessities must be protected from seizure, how long recovery remains owed, and whether an uncollectable balance is eventually discharged. Do not recover more than the restitution paid or add collection charges without a separate lawful basis. Inability to pay must not become compelled work, confinement or automatic exclusion from services. Allocation of payment between governments and currencies remains linked to P34/P35. T52–T53 test the present assignment and limits; no insolvency scheme is adopted.

**Hearings (5 November):** T53 (no forced work to repay) agreed. T52 agreed that the recovery service cannot finally decide its own disputed demand; it split only on `forbiddable`. The insolvency questions above remain.

**Trimmed (5 November), not yet heard:** III.2.3 now states only the principle: the harmed are paid at once from public funds, and the government that paid recovers it. A recovery service and court route are matters for law, within II.1.3 and III.2.8.

## Open choices from drafting

### P13. Killing to eat

The starvation condition on II.2.1 is species-blind, so any condition letting someone kill an animal to eat also lets them kill a person to eat, and III.1.2 rules out a lower bar for animals. Keep the bar at death, lower it for everyone, or drop the condition.

**Hearings (5 November):** a probe in which a starving Arlo kills Bea and eats her split, and the split shows a new edge. Every run found the killing permitted by the condition. But Haiku held that eating the body breaches II.1.4, an absolute right with no starvation exception, since Bea gave no directions; one Luna run found the same unclear. As written, the condition permits killing to eat but not eating, so it either does nothing or II.1.4 needs a matching exception.

### P14. The coroner

Article II.1.4 now protects the deceased's directions, with a limited court fallback under 8.10. This replaces the named person's unrestricted control. No compulsory investigative examination is authorised merely because it would be useful. Test T7 still lacks the deceased's examination directions and remains undecided on those facts.

**Third hearings:** as predicted, both hearings held that the named person controls the body and the coroner may not examine it. Both flagged that a possible killing then can't be investigated.

**Discussion (4 November):** prefer exploring a freely chosen sequence of custodians over assuming a compulsory coroner exception. A person could name a coroner for a limited examination, then someone else for care or disposal, with substitutes if either dies or cannot act. A nomination to an office needs a rule making it pass to the next office-holder; an individual's death cannot silently transfer the body to their heirs. Do not treat custody as unrestricted ownership, or give the custodian power to defeat the deceased's directions.

Open: the default when no effective nomination survives; how a nomination is recorded and revoked; how duress is checked; and whether a successor may authorise anything the deceased did not. The Negative Privileges Advocate is a possible default, but IV.4.3 does not expressly extend its role beyond death. Test examination within and beyond a recorded nomination, then the death of the first custodian with and without a surviving substitute. Keep T7 undecided until this policy is chosen.

**Applied draft (4 November), not yet heard:** II.1.4 and 8.10 now protect directions, allow successive nominees and office-holders, and permit a court to authorise only the nearest lawful, practicable replacement for an unworkable direction. Other workable directions survive; refusing examination does not itself justify overriding that refusal. With no directions found, appointment is limited to safe care and disposal. The court hears reachable nominees and the Negative Rights Advocate, gives reasons and allows review. Directions obtained by duress or deception are void under I.2.5. No one can be compelled to serve under II.1.3. Still open: recording and finding directions, urgent care before a court can act, disputed cost or practicability, and irreconcilable directions. T47–T51 test the new boundaries; P14 remains open.

**Procedure extracted (4 November), not yet heard:** 8.10 now invokes Instrument 2 for nomination, succession and the court fallback, with safe care and disposal as the default purpose and the Negative Rights Advocate heard. The examination-refusal safeguard remains in 8.10. This is the Instrument's only application; it grants no custody over living patients. Any later use for nominated care or decisions for a living patient needs separate authority and safeguards under P47, including consent, revocation and the existing protection against confinement.

**Hearings (5 November):** the applied procedure holds. T47 and T48 agreed as intended. T49–T51 agreed on the outcome, but split on `forbiddable`, as the question fits these cases badly. In T51 one run read the coroner's request itself as a breach. T7 split between no breach and unclear, as expected for facts deliberately left open. What remains is drafting detail (recording directions, urgent care, cost, conflicting directions), not the principle.

**Folded (5 November), not yet heard:** 8.10 is gone; II.1.4 invokes Instrument 2 directly. The sentence "a refusal of examination is not itself an unworkable direction" is dropped as implied: Instrument 2 replaces only a direction that cannot be followed, and a refusal always can.

### P15. Incitement

Urging a crowd to harm someone may or may not count as "a threat of harm" under III.1.3. The intended answer is that only the attackers answer, but the text doesn't say so clearly. Test T17 is unclear.

**Third hearings:** the attackers breached II.2.1 in both hearings; the speaker's position was unclear in both. One hearing suggested another route to the speaker: urging a crowd may put the victim at unreasonable risk (II.2.2).

**Hearings (5 November):** T17 agreed that the attackers breach II.2.1. The speaker was not found to breach anything, but runs still asked whether "teach a lesson" is a threat of harm under III.1.3, so the speaker's position is left to the facts rather than settled. That may be the right place for it.

**Applied (8 November), not yet heard:** the II.2 defence rule now excludes only suffering caused by expression, so speech about to cause imminent death or injury, such as urging a crowd on, may be stopped as far as necessary and in proportion. This lets the speaker be stopped without anything they say being a breach.

### P16. Enforcing risk rules without punishment

A speed limit is valid, because speeding puts others at risk, but with no punishment and no harm done, it's unclear how a limit is enforced at all. Test T2 notes this.

**Third hearings: test T2 failed.** Both hearings found that speeding on an empty street breaches nothing, so a speed limit can't forbid it, because risk was judged act by act. A general rule against a kind of risky act needs explicit permission, for instance: law may forbid a kind of act that generally puts patients at unreasonable risk, even where a particular instance harms no one.

**Hearings (5 November): T2 still fails.** Haiku found no breach; Luna was unclear. A probe stating the law's general-risk rationale (40 mph at 3 a.m., no one about) fared worse: Luna held the limit itself a breach by nearlaw, reading II.2's defence rule's imminent-danger test as the only ground for limiting liberty. The general-rule permission remains needed.

### P17. The Advocates

Article IV.4.4 now makes each Advocate an office, headed by a body of three volunteers chosen by Instrument 1, one facing reconfirmation each year, with 25 years' maximum service (P49). Not yet heard. The Advocates began as a bureau of their own; how each office is staffed below its head is open. An Advocate may bring a claim on behalf of any patient under III.2.7, but other powers need work: whether they can inspect, intervene, or compel a government to act.

### P18. Conditions on public speech and broadcast

Article II.8.1 has no stated conditions; as a privilege, it may still be waived. Whether any conditions should apply to equitable public free speech and broadcast, and what they should be, remains undecided. P60 asks what "equitable" means.

### P19. Citizenship

Which government grants and records citizenship, on what criteria, and who handles renunciation. Naturalisation looks like a crossing, which would make it farlaw's.

Citizen records now also determine the size of the bodies under Instrument 1. Survey, registration, count dates and corrections remain open; see P49. The sizing rule does not itself assign citizenship administration to either government.

### P21. False reports to the authorities

Nothing addresses someone knowingly reporting innocent conduct to the authorities, or what follows if the authorities act on it.

**Hearings (5 November):** confirmed. When Hana falsely reports Tom for theft and police confine him for a day, every run found nearlaw in breach for the confinement. Hana's report is expression, and a lie is no exception under III.1.3, so she answers for nothing; Haiku left that unclear rather than settled.

### P22. Officials' duty to act

An inspector who sees harm and does nothing breaches nothing as written. Whether people acting in a government's name have duties to act, not just limits, is undecided.

### P23. What counts as possessed

Unclear cases: cash in a till, which a worker holds for the shop; bank balances; digital accounts and messages; and whether copying something takes it when the original remains.

**Third hearings:** the bank scam was unclear in both hearings, solely because money in an account may not be a "thing" Jo holds. Also raised: whether cloud storage and data can be held, whether a sealed parcel is a communication or record, and who has a claim to stolen goods a third person bought.

**Direction (4 November):** do not add special constitutional protection for bank balances or make promises held things merely to satisfy T16. Claims on a bank or a points scheme do not become possessions by assumption (P51). Physical cash, direct control of digital assets, custody for another and collective use need separate treatment; direct control alone must not make theft confer a claim. Holding should protect a toothbrush or phone through ordinary absence without turning every loan into a perpetual income-bearing title. The present inclusion of lent things is an edge to test, not a settled answer against absentee ownership.

**Candidate, not applied:** alongside P3's shared-holding wording, add "Land and buildings can be claimed only by occupancy." This prevents a movable thing's continuing holding rule from becoming an alternative land title. It does not settle whether a fitted door or lift can be claimed separately to defeat occupation of an otherwise empty building (P5). Do not introduce a use-it-or-lose-it rule for personal possessions merely to address absentee landholding.

**Proposed cases:** personal use versus custody for another; a cooperative's shared tool versus an outside investor's claim; and a deceptive transfer of physical cash versus a bank balance versus loyalty points. Keep any cryptocurrency case separate: possession of a key, consent to transfer and competing claims must be specified, not assumed settled.

**Hearings (5 November):** T16 (bank scam) now agrees as a breach of II.2.6. A loyalty-points scam also agreed as a breach, though Haiku asked whether points are held at all. The models are treating claims on a bank or scheme as held things by assumption, which is exactly what the direction above warns against; the text has not decided it. Cash taken from a till was a breach in every run, with Haiku asking whether the worker or the shop holds it.

## Decided in the corpus, not yet in the constitution

These were endorsed in the corpus (archived in wroxia-archive.zip) but never carried over.

### P24. No tax on labour or exchange

Under MMT, taxes needn't fund the state, so they can be confined to land, crossings and harms. Article IV.2.3 limits each government to its own domain but doesn't rule out income or sales taxes.

**Hearings (5 November):** a 10% wage tax was not itself found unlawful: every run read III.1.4 as permitting taxes. See P58 for the larger finding that no tax can be collected without consent.

### P25. Land value tax and the land allowance

Nearlaw's tax is never named. The birthright allowance (pay LVT only on value above your per-person share) and deferral for the land-rich, cash-poor were left open, and the allowance now also underpins the community land-pricing idea in P31.

### P26. Territory and conquest

Two rules were firmly endorsed: territory where farlaw holds the monopoly on violence gains its own nearlaw, and annexed populations get votes in farlaw.

### P27. Labour-hours, upheld not enforced

An opt-in economy paid by the hour, which no one is compelled into. It may fit better as one kind of community under P31 than as a function of either government.

### P28. Home defence

Nearlaw's militia handles defence of home, and farlaw only borders and expeditions, so a trade-funded farlaw limits foreign adventures, not defence. The constitution only says each government may keep forces.

### P29. Amending the constitution

There is no amendment process at all. Entrenchment, meaning a supermajority or a long delay for core changes, was the corpus's answer to the law changing underfoot.

## Bigger design questions

### P30. Who leads each government

Neither government has a head or a way of choosing one. One idea is a Steward for nearlaw and a Warden for farlaw, chosen by election, by lot, or a mix. Article 10 on rendering has since been folded into Positive (I.1.8); law-making is now IV.3, which leaders could join.

### P31. A third relationship: chosen communities

Farlaw contains dangerous powers but is small in daily life, so it gives citizens little plurality. One framing is three relationships: to the world (farlaw), to the land (nearlaw, slimmed to land, commons and shared courts) and to one another (communities people choose to join). Communities could make consensual rules, run their own services, economy and currency, and settle members' disputes, with exit always open and the universal rights binding them all.

A community could settle unpossessed land, paying land value tax on any area beyond its members' combined allowances, so its size tracks its population through price rather than redrawn borders. Unresolved: most "unclaimed" land is where wild animals live; who judges disputes between members of different communities; when children can choose to leave; and whether people belong to one community or several.

**Proposal: four relationships (with P3)**

Every person relates to four bodies, each on a different basis, and none has a total claim on them.

1. **Farlaw: the world.** Unchanged.
2. **Nearlaw: rights and land.** Shrinks to the shared courts, support for the Advocates, land value tax with the land allowance, and the near currency. It owns almost nothing itself.
3. **Commons: where you live.** Small geographic bodies holding streets, parks, water and other shared things, with by-laws made by their own residents, perhaps in rotation chosen by lot. Membership is simply living there, so each commons grows and shrinks with its population on its own. Each is funded by a share of the land value tax raised in its area, so it gains when more people live there and the land is worth more. This follows Elinor Ostrom's findings: the users make the rules, watch over them, and settle disputes locally. It answers P3.
4. **Communities: who you choose.** Voluntary associations, with or without land, that can run services, an internal economy (the labour-hour economy in P27 is one candidate), dues and their own currency, and settle disputes among members. Exit is always open, the universal rights bind them all, and disputes between members of different communities go to nearlaw's courts.

**Settling land.** A community may settle unoccupied land, paying land value tax on any area beyond the combined allowances of members who actually live there. It may not move any patient from where they live (Article II.2.3), so it must find land where no animal lives, or share it with those who do.

**What it answers.** Plurality: four relationships resting on the world, land, place and choice. Proportional size: commons by residence, community land by price. Opting out (P37): anyone can leave every community, while the commons and nearlaw remain as a floor.

**Footprint.** Two short articles, Commons and Communities, plus a line giving commons their share of land value tax.

**Still open:** one community or several; how commons and communities take part in law-making (P36); when a child can leave a community; and the least a commons must provide.

**Remarks (2 November):** needs very deep thought before anything is drafted. Two named dangers: small communities turning into the worst kind of homeowners' association, and raids, where a community is flooded with new members to swing an outcome.

### P32. Farlaw's revenue

If merchants fund farlaw through customs while every citizen votes for it, funders and voters are different people. Options include fees on crossings of people and a far share of land tax, since defence protects land.

### P33. Visitors

Whether cases involving non-residents go to a farlaw magistrate and police, as Rome's praetor peregrinus heard cases involving foreigners, or stay with nearlaw like everyone else's.

**Hearings (5 November):** a tourist stealing a Wroxian's bicycle went to nearlaw in every run, the intended default. Haiku still asked whether a foreign visitor is "a party outside Wroxia" under IV.2.1.1; the answer turns on P52.

### P34. Paying for shared institutions

The Advocates and the boundary court belong to neither government. Which currency pays them, and who raises it?

### P35. The two currencies, simulated

Tests E1 to E4 describe what the two currencies should do under trade shocks and money printing. None can pass or fail until there's a simulation to run them in.

## From the project's starting points

Aims set out at the very beginning, and ideas from early conversation, that the constitution hasn't yet dealt with.

### P36. How law is made

Articles IV.3.1–IV.3.3 now establish Nearlaw's law-making body by sticky sortition, Farlaw's elected law-makers and a separate sticky-sortition veto body. Each decides by Instrument 4: a majority of those voting, with more than half its seats taking part. Election and legislative procedures remain unfinished. The founding worry remains relevant: a democracy where every vote can lose, and the law changes underfoot regardless.

**Options:**

1. **Elected assemblies for each government.** Familiar and plainly legitimate, but it leaves the founding complaint untouched: you can vote all your life and always lose.
2. **Citizens' assemblies chosen by lot.** Laws are drafted and passed by a randomly chosen cross-section of citizens, with a referendum only when enough people petition against a law. No parties, no campaigns and no permanent losers, but less expertise, and members answer to no one.
3. **Different methods for each government.** Nearlaw's laws made by citizens chosen by lot; farlaw led by elected people, since judging diplomacy and defence by results is what elections do well, with a lot-chosen body that can veto. This mirrors Athens, which chose most officials by lot but elected its generals. Fits each job, but it's the most complex.

**Suggested:** option 3. Whatever is chosen, the constitution itself should need more than ordinary law-making to change (P29).

**Decision (2 November):** for farlaw, elected representatives curtailed by a veto group chosen by lot, for now. Sortition is welcome only where it clearly fits, not as a default. Nearlaw's method is still open.

**Idea for bodies chosen by lot (2 November):** "sticky sortition". Empty seats are filled by lot; each sitting member is scored individually by the public, from 0 to 5, and keeps their seat with an average of 2.5 or more. A fixed bar per member, not a ranking, so members aren't rivals. Precedents for parts of it: Venice's doge elections alternating lot and election; the Ostbelgien Citizens' Council's staggered rotation; Missouri-plan retention votes for judges, which rarely remove anyone. Settled for now: scores from 0 to 5 (six levels, within the range rating-scale research favours, and the scale STAR voting uses), keeping a seat at an average of 2.5 or more, and a maximum of 25 years in total, to be whittled down later. Still to settle: a minimum number of ratings before anyone can be removed. Candidates: farlaw's veto body and the Advocates.

**Discussion (4 November):** consider authorising a legislature to supply missing procedures, not to decide individual cases or suspend constitutional limits. Article III.1.4 already permits institutions and ordinary law within those limits. Sticky sortition would choose office-holders, not enlarge their powers. Instrument 1 still needs article-supplied parameters before use; no legislature or selection method is adopted by this discussion. Preserve the earlier farlaw election/veto choice unless it is expressly replaced.

**Applied draft (4 November), not yet heard:** both sortition bodies use Instrument 1 with 25 years' maximum service, annual reconfirmation and one third facing each round. Nearlaw's body targets the cube root of the number of citizens and Farlaw's veto body half that, each rounded down to a multiple of three with a minimum of three, and both resize under the Instrument. This supersedes the earlier fixed-size and exponent-0.3 proposals. P49 holds the unfinished Instrument mechanics.

**Still open:** how Farlaw's representatives are elected (P67); how proposals are introduced, published and amended; the time allowed for veto and what happens if the veto body cannot act; deadlock; and the relationship between law-making and executive offices (P30). These provisions give no power to override universal protections or the other government's jurisdiction. They do not adopt an amendment process (P29). T54–T55 test the basic allocation only.

**Hearings (5 November):** T54 and T55 agreed that the bodies are lawfully constituted and that the veto takes effect, but split on outcome, `forbiddable` and, for T55, jurisdiction. The hearing's questions fit institutional cases poorly: an official refusing to recognise a body limits no privilege, so "breach" has no clear meaning. The models also did not agree whether a veto of farlaw's law is farlaw's matter. Treat these as structural checks rather than rights cases.

### P37. Opting out

The original aim was a state you can opt out of. Renouncing citizenship is allowed, but it's unclear what changes: whether a non-citizen living in Wroxia is still bound by its law, what someone who opts out of everything owes for the land they live on, and in which currency.

**Hearings (5 November):** one part is settled. Someone who renounces citizenship but stays and punches a neighbour breaches II.2.1 (one run unclear), because the universal privileges bind every agent, citizen or not. Land and currency obligations remain open.

### P38. Due process

Articles III.2.8–III.2.9 now require an independent hearing of disputed breach claims, a chance to answer the claim and evidence, reasons, one review of the claim at each party's request by another court, and prompt review of involuntary confinement or removal. Still open: the general burden and standard of proof, how evidence is gathered and returned, time limits, and what happens to someone wrongly accused while a case is open. The new confinement review provision places the burden of justifying confinement or removal on whoever imposed it, but grants no power to gather evidence by limiting other privileges. How repeated reviews of continuing confinement work remains open; III.2.8 does not grant an endless chain of appeals.

**Third hearings:** in the seized-car case both the detention and holding the car stood as breaches, as intended. But it shows that, as written, any investigation that holds a person or a thing is a breach, since no condition covers gathering evidence.

**Discussion (4 November):** ordinary law can supply hearings, evidence rules, reasons and review under III.1.4, but cannot create a confinement, search or seizure exception absent from the relevant privilege. An undecided fact is different from an undecided rule: a court needs a way to find facts; the legislature may fill procedural gaps only within the Constitution. Priorities are an independent decision-maker, a chance to answer the claim, reasons, a burden of proof, timely review and a remedy for wrongful intervention. Choosing their exact form remains open.

**Hearings (5 November):** confirmed starkly. Police keep a laptop for a week as evidence with a court's approval after hearing both sides, and the suspect is later found to have breached II.2.6. Every run still found the seizure a breach by nearlaw, since no condition of II.2.6 covers evidence and a court's approval is not one. As written, a court can decide a case but nothing can be held to prove it.

### P39. Inheritance and accumulation

Nothing says what happens to possessions at death. Land passes to whoever lives or works on it, but money and other things are unaddressed. The founding aim was to prevent wealth concentrating rather than redistribute it afterwards; possession and land value tax do this for land, but nothing yet does it for money.

**Hearings (5 November):** confirmed. A son taking cash left in his late mother's drawer, with no directions, came out unclear in every run.

### P40. Groups as agents

Governments answer as agents, but nothing says whether communities, companies or other organisations can possess things, make agreements or breach rights. The early question of mobs, where a crowd seems to act with agency no single member holds, sits here too.

**Third hearings:** hacking a former employer's cloud storage was unclear in both hearings, largely over whether a company can hold anything or be harmed. Its clients' contact lists may still be protected as records about them (II.2.7).

**Hearings (5 November):** the same hack now agrees as a breach of II.2.7, as copying records about the clients. Haiku added that the company itself, not being sentient, holds no privilege. Individuals are protected through records about them; whether an organisation can hold, occupy or be harmed remains open.

### P41. Exclusion as a sanction

An early idea, from the excludable and non-excludable distinction: the state's only lever against someone is withholding excludable things it provides, such as transit or services, never their body, speech or privacy. It may be one answer to P11 and P16.

### P42. Cars and unreasonable risk

Whether ordinary driving puts pedestrians at unreasonable risk under II.2.2, who decides, and whether streets as commons (P3) change the answer. The founding preference for walking, cycling and transit has no foothold yet beyond these.

**Hearings (5 November):** confirmed. Driving at the legal limit along a narrow shopping street, so that pedestrians must step aside, came out unclear in every run.

### P44. Adversarial testing

Only two of the three planned testing layers have run: fixed tests and repeated hearings. The third, an agent rewarded for finding outcomes that are legal but awful, hasn't been tried.

### P45. A length budget

The aim from the start was a constitution short enough to hold in one mind or one context window. There's no agreed limit yet, so nothing stops it growing clause by clause.

## Added 2 November

### P46. The judicial side

I.1.14 now defines a court as independent of the parties; III.2.8–III.2.9 give a minimal hearing and review process. Still open: who judges, selection and funding, the detailed procedure, what orders a court may make, and its relationship with the boundary court and the Advocates. A former proposal for later preventive confinement depended on a judicial case (P11); the present defence condition does not adopt that proposal. This overlaps with P38 on due process.

**Third hearings:** two questions for the judicial side came up: who declares that a bribed official has lost their role (III.2.6), and how quickly a joint occupant who causes harm can be separated (P48).

**Discussion (4 November):** keep making general law distinct from deciding an individual case, even if both bodies eventually use sortition. A governing assembly must not be able to authorise its own otherwise unlawful detention. Define a court in Part I before relying on it in a protective condition, and specify its independence and review route. Allocation between nearlaw and farlaw concerns the matter under Article IV.2, not a separate set of universal protections: domestic location alone does not exclude an outside party, and being abroad does not confer lawful power under the host country's law.

### P47. Nominated trust

An idea from P11 that reaches well beyond it: each person nominates whom decisions about them are deferred to, such as family, friends or coworkers. Possible uses include decisions for someone who can't consent, children adding nominees as they grow, and restorative conferences.

Remarks: for this to shape how others behave towards you, it has to be easily knowable who you're dealing with and whom they've nominated, as when knowing someone's father would step in makes people behave differently. Geography makes norms legible: in a given place you know how to behave. Functional, non-geographic arrangements blur those lines, so that legibility needs watching.

Known risks: coercion into a nomination, voided by duress but needing nominations to be private and revocable; people with no one to nominate, needing a default such as an Advocate; and concentration, if nominations ever aggregate into public offices, which turns them into a popularity contest.

### P48. Abuse in a shared home

Article II.2's shared defence condition now permits necessary and proportionate intervention against imminent death, injury or suffering, including restraint or removal. It creates no general power to exclude a joint occupant after that threat ends, even after a judicial case. Someone facing repeated but not imminent harm may therefore still have to leave unless another lawful measure stops it. Restitution in kind can pay for somewhere else to live, but does not itself remove the person causing harm. A first draft gave orders that could move someone out of their home without evidence, and was withdrawn for that reason. Later protection still needs work with P11/P38/P46.

**Discussion (4 November):** consider this alongside P38/P46 when deciding what ordinary law may supply. A legislature can establish a process, but cannot authorise removal contrary to II.2.3 merely by calling it protective. T31/T32 now test actual removal and confinement separately from Ana's request in T25. A future protective condition must explicitly change those expectations rather than quietly treating the old cases as passing.

### P49. Instruments

Instrument 2 now holds the reusable directions-and-custody procedure, applied only to bodies after death by II.1.4. Relevant cases must include it, not just the articles. This extends the earlier office-selection-only scope of Instruments noted below. Possible broader applications remain open under P47; extraction is not permission for any other kind of custody.

Proposal: operational detail such as voting methods, scales, thresholds and terms lives in appendices called instruments, keeping the articles short (P45). The open question is how instruments change. Anything governing how power is won or kept, such as voting systems and terms, should be as hard to change as the constitution itself, or only take effect after the next election or selection, so no one can rewrite the rules for their own benefit. Purely administrative instruments could change more easily. Links to P29 on amendment.

**Decision (2 November):** every part of every voting system, and constants such as the 0 to 5 scale, the 2.5 average and the 25-year limit, are enshrined in the constitution with the same protection as the articles, but kept in an appendix of instruments that the articles point to. An arbiter judging an ordinary case reads the articles only; instruments matter only when a case is about how someone came to hold office.

**Sticky sortition, as understood so far:** at each round every sitting member faces a retention score vote alone, against a fixed bar rather than other candidates, so STAR doesn't apply. Anyone averaging below 2.5, reaching 25 years' total service, resigning or dying leaves, and every empty seat is filled by lot. Settled and drafted as Instrument 1: no one is voted in, only reconfirmed; rounds are staggered for continuity; empty seats go first to volunteers who weren't just voted out, then to those who were, highest score first; no minimum turnout, since keeping the electorate engaged is part of the job. Still open: term length, the share reconfirmed each round, what record voters judge members by, and whether to draw volunteers in proportion to the population's makeup.

**Current application (4 November), not yet heard:** Articles IV.3.1–IV.3.2 now use Instrument 1. Maximum service is 25 years; rounds are annual and one third face reconfirmation. The target is the cube root of citizen numbers rounded down to a multiple of three, minimum three. Growth moves halfway from current size towards target, rounded down to threes; contraction retires only empty seats, no more than half the vacancies and no further than target. Remaining vacancies are filled by lot. This supersedes earlier notes on unset timing, fraction and filling every vacated seat.

**Applied (8 November), not yet heard:** Instrument 1 now takes _seats_, so each article sets its own size, and _cohorts_ in place of _reconfirmed fraction_: the number of seats divided by _cohorts_ face each round, and sizes are multiples of _cohorts_. Nearlaw's body takes the cube root of citizen numbers, Farlaw's veto body half that, and each Advocate's head three seats; all use three cohorts.

**Unfinished mechanics, parked:** establish the initial three reconfirmation groups and resolve ties in time since reconfirmation; allocate new members across groups after expansion and replacement; handle insufficient volunteers, members with no scores, and the existing highest-score-first wording alongside selection by lot. Deliberate stalling below the target through rounding is accepted, not an allowance carried forward. Citizen survey and registration, counting citizens abroad or unable to act, the measurement date and how a disputed count is corrected need work with P19. Decide how changes in the count reach the next round and how death or resignation between rounds is handled. No further Instrument rules are adopted here.

### P50. Guards against farlaw drifting inward

Carried over from the old "Nearlaw and farlaw" write-up. The early United States, whose federal government began much like farlaw (war, foreign relations, foreign trade and naturalisation, funded mostly by customs), grew inward by three routes: ever-broader readings of its commerce power, a domestic income tax from 1913, and wartime expansion. Of the guards proposed against those routes, only some are in the constitution:

- **In:** each force enforces only its own government's law (IV.2.4); matters are nearlaw's unless shown to be far (IV.2.5); messages are far only while crossing (IV.2.1).
- **Not in:** emergencies never change which matters are near or far; farlaw taxes only goods, people and money physically crossing the border, where IV.2.3 now says only "matters within its own domain"; and territory farlaw comes to hold gains its own nearlaw (P26).
- **A choice to make:** farlaw's scope is currently a test (an outside party, or a crossing) rather than a list of powers. A list is easier to police; a test follows far work wherever it appears.

**Clarification (4 November):** the Constitution grants no emergency power to alter jurisdiction. The proposed emergency sentence would be an express guard against an expansive future reading, not the repair of an existing emergency exception. Leave it unapplied; test the existing boundary before adding redundant text.

The write-up also listed what farlaw would do in practice: diplomacy and treaties, defence, entry and exit, passports and probably naturalisation, customs, care of Wroxians abroad, embassies and counter-espionage, and territory beyond Wroxia. Defence relies on volunteers, since no one may be compelled to work (II.1.3), which matters most in wartime.

## Added after the third hearings

### P51. Promises and debts

Nothing in the constitution makes a promise or contract binding. In the landlord case both hearings rightly found Alex has no claim to rent, but for the wrong general reason: unpaid money is owed under no right or privilege at all. The same would apply to wages, loans and sales on credit. Either breaking a freely made promise needs to count as a breach, perhaps through holding (what was promised is owed), or Wroxia deliberately has no enforceable contracts.

**Direction (4 November):** leave general contractual enforcement open. Do not introduce it indirectly through bank balances or an expanded definition of Holding (P23). Any later protection for particular promises must preserve the rejection of absentee land claims, compelled work and consent obtained by duress or deception.

### P52. Acting in a role, or personally

The off-duty diplomat's assault left jurisdiction unclear in both hearings: does someone who acts for an outside power make it a party to everything they do? A likely fix is that an outside power is a party only to what is done in its name. The same idea settled the gum sale (formerly P8).

**Hearings (5 November): T3 still splits.** Luna put the off-duty assault under nearlaw, since nothing shows the outside power is a party; Haiku put it under farlaw, treating the diplomat himself as "a party outside Wroxia" under IV.2.1.1. The proposed fix stands. A tourist stealing a bicycle went to nearlaw in every run, but Haiku asked the same question about whether a foreign visitor is an outside party (see P33).

### P54. Disclosure, in place of keys and passwords

Encryption is protected only indirectly today: II.1.2 bars compelling "any key or password", II.2.7 protects confidences, and III.1.1 stops encryption being forbidden. Unlike a lock, encryption can't be forced; it travels with copies of the data rather than staying in a place; copying sealed data takes nothing; and the only way in is the person who withholds the key. So the primal concept is withholding, of which silence about what you know is the oldest form.

Proposed framing: information has three states. **Withheld** (in your head, or encrypted) is as private as a thought. **Confided** (disclosed to someone in particular) stays with them. **Public** (freely disclosed) is free, and copying it breaches nothing, which is the piracy position.

**Proposed case pairs (4 November):** a recording of a remark freely made at a public meeting versus the same remark confided privately; separately, a leaked intimate record versus one freely published by its subject. Do not equate public availability after a breach with the subject's consent to publication. Keep the public remark's protection under III.1.3 distinct from the recording question.

> **Disclosure** (noun): making information knowable to another. **Right:** Not to be made, by force or deceit, to disclose or give access to anything they withhold, nor to have their means of withholding weakened. **Privilege (II.2.7, reframed):** What one has disclosed in confidence may not be passed on without one's consent.

**Hearings (5 November):** T1 (encryption ban) agreed that the ban has no force. In T6 one run of each model read "police demand" as mere expression, needing duress before II.1.2 is breached; II.1.2 says "compelled" without defining it. Police pressing a suspect's finger to her phone's reader agreed as a breach, but through II.2.7 (reading her messages), with Haiku asking whether a fingerprint is a "key". Both gaps support the withholding framing above.

This names no keys or passwords, yet covers passwords, fingerprint unlocks, thoughts, phishing and laws requiring backdoors. US courts distinguish surrendering a strongbox key from revealing a safe's combination; the test here is simply whether something withheld is being taken. Open: whether proving authorship (digital signatures) belongs here too, as the other half of the same mathematics.

**Restructure (6 November), not yet heard:** II.1.2 now reads "Not to be compelled to reveal, or give access to, information they withhold." "Key or password" and "private communications" are gone; T58–T59 probe biometrics. Hear with T1 and T6.

### P55. Government secrecy

Governments aren't patients, so as written they can keep nothing private, and leaking war plans breaches nothing. Proposed layers: (1) disclosing what puts people at unreasonable risk breaches II.2.2, so law may forbid it; records about individuals are already protected by II.2.7; (2) an official who discloses what they were entrusted to keep loses the role, as with corruption under III.2.6; (3) everything else, including misconduct, waste and embarrassment, may be disclosed freely, protecting whistleblowers. Secrecy is justified by protecting people, never by the state's own interest. Links to P40 on organisations.

**Hearings (5 November):** confirmed. A farlaw official giving border-defence plans to a newspaper split between no breach and unclear. Neither III.2.6 (role loss requires accepting value) nor II.2.7 (records about patients) reached it, and II.2.2 was raised only as an unproven risk.

**When many share a secret:** each person entrusted holds it in confidence separately, and discussing it among themselves isn't disclosure. Only the person who leaks loses their role; there is no collective sanction. Once leaked, the information is public, so those who repeat it breach nothing, unless repeating it puts people at unreasonable risk, which II.2.2 forbids for anyone. Knowing a secret is never evidence of having leaked it: a leak may be anonymous, snooped from a device or the result of a hack, even when only one person was entrusted. So role loss requires proof of the act itself, with the burden on the organisation, as in III.2.5. Most leaks will therefore be unattributable, and secrets must be protected mainly by prevention rather than sanctions. Where an anonymous leak endangers people, the fund in III.2.3 still pays those harmed. A hack from abroad has an outside party, so it is farlaw's matter. Organisations can protect themselves without law: giving each person a subtly different copy (a "canary trap") identifies a leak's source, and splitting a secret cryptographically so several holders are needed to reveal it (Shamir's secret sharing, 1979; the "two-person rule") means no one person can leak it.

### P56. Where occupancy ends and holding begins

Articles II.2.5 and II.2.6 protect occupancy and holding respectively, but do not settle whether land or buildings, or their fitted doors and lifts, can also be held as things. Part I's application rules allow claims to land and buildings only by occupancy, while holding continues until a thing is given away or discarded. A claim to a fixture could therefore defeat occupancy of an otherwise empty building, or give an absent person control of a shared place. This gathers the boundary question from P5/P23; P3/P31/P40 retain the separate questions of commons and collective decisions.

The split into separate occupancy and holding privileges clarifies which acts and conditions belong to each, but does not itself decide what can be held. Do not silently settle that question by adding "movable" to Holding or excluding every fixture: either could remove protection without replacing it.

**Applied editorial split (4 November), not yet heard:** occupancy is now II.2.5 and holding II.2.6. Both refer to defence under II.2; taking for restitution is a condition only on holding. Privacy and inability to consent are now II.2.7 and II.2.8. No boundary between buildings, fixtures and held things has been decided.

**Proposed cases:** compare a detached door kept for repair with a door fitted to an unoccupied building; compare a lift maintained for its users with an absent financier's claim to it. Decide how damage and taking are protected without creating an absentee land claim. No new property rule is adopted.

**Hearings (5 November): the fixture loophole is real.** Paolo forces the fitted door of an office no one has used for years, and the company that built it claims it still holds the door. Three of four runs found a breach of II.2.6 with restitution owed: the company never gave the door away or discarded it, so it still holds it. Absent owners keep a foothold in empty buildings through their fixtures, which is what I.2.3 is meant to prevent. T23, which does not mention an owner, mostly passes only because no one asserts a claim.

**Terms restructuring (4 November), not yet heard:** Part I now separates Definitions from Application. Definitions retain their numbers; the sentience presumption, agency threshold, occupancy and holding rules, and invalidity of coerced or deceptive consent have moved into Application. Lending still preserves holding. This restructuring does not resolve P56; the later editorial split is recorded above.

### P57. Establishing capacity

I.2.2 presumes a human to have been an agent when acting, unless evidence shows otherwise. Instrument 3 is a stub for establishing that capacity; it supplies no assessment procedure, compulsory examination or power to confine. The presumption itself (formerly P20) passed T56–T57 at the 5 November hearings; this Problem concerns how agency is established in a case, and when a child becomes an agent.

Decide what evidence can rebut the presumption, who must produce it, who bears the ultimate burden and what standard of proof applies. A bare assertion of incapacity, refusal to cooperate and a diagnosis are not interchangeable with evidence of inability to understand others' rights and privileges or act on that understanding. Capacity at the time of the act must be distinguished from capacity to take part in a later hearing, consent to care or make a particular decision. Consider fluctuating capacity, intoxication and non-human patients without silently changing the definition of Agent.

The presumption currently includes children. Decide whether developmental capacity needs a different starting point or procedure, rather than importing an undefined adulthood threshold. Assessments should consider relevant conduct and other evidence without requiring the person's cooperation to establish capacity. Choose independent assessment, a chance to challenge its evidence, privacy and review safeguards, and how assessment costs are met. None of those procedures is adopted by the Instrument stub.

Responsibility and protection remain separate: incapacity does not remove a patient's entitlements, and a finding of incapacity does not itself permit confinement, forced work, treatment or disclosure. Existing defence and review provisions still apply. Cross-references: P11/P38/P46/P47/P48. T56–T57 are initial boundaries, not coverage of all these choices.

**Hearings (5 November):** T56 agreed. T57 agreed once the prompt told models to read cases plainly; before that, Luna once refused to accept rebuttal at all, because Instrument 3 "provides no procedure". Haiku cites the empty Instrument 3 as a doubt in many unrelated cases, so the stub itself adds noise. A six-year-old deliberately breaking a classmate's toy was found in breach in three of four runs: the presumption reaches young children unless someone brings evidence otherwise.

## Added 5 November

### P60. What "equitable" means in II.8.1

Article II.8.1 grants "equitable public free speech and broadcast" but never says what makes it equitable, and no earlier draft recorded the intent. The word could mean any of:

1. **Equal shares.** Every citizen gets the same access to public platforms, as with equal airtime for candidates. Simple and checkable, but most citizens will not use their share, and those who do may want far more.
2. **Neutral allocation.** A government may allocate its platforms by any fair method, such as lot, queue or need, but never by viewpoint, party or standing. This constrains only the allocator, not outcomes.
3. **A floor.** Every citizen has some real means of reaching the public, however small, even if others reach far more.
4. **A ceiling.** No one, however rich or popular, may dominate public attention. Closest to the founding worry about money drowning out votes, but hardest to square with III.1.1 and III.1.3: limiting one person's reach forbids expression that breaches nothing.

Because II.8.1 is positive, it is fulfilled only by action of the government in whose domain it falls (I.1.8). So a private newspaper refusing a letter cannot breach it, and "equitable" likely constrains only what governments provide or allocate: their own broadcasters, public noticeboards and spaces, and perhaps radio spectrum. Whether governments must provide anything at all, and which one owes broadcast that crosses the border, are open. "Public free speech" may also add nothing to III.1.3 unless it means a place or channel to speak in.

The spirit needs stating before the word can be tested: is II.8.1 about a fair process of allocation (2), or about the shape of public attention (1, 3 or 4)? See P18 on conditions.

**Proposed cases:** a public broadcaster gives the governing body's members more airtime than its critics; a town allocates its only noticeboard first come, first served, and one group fills it daily; a government platform down-ranks a citizen with a million followers to equalise reach; spectrum auctioned to the highest bidder; a private newspaper refuses a letter.

### P61. Enclosure

England's enclosures turned shared rights to graze, gather fuel and cross land into one owner's exclusive title, usually by Act of Parliament, pushing commoners off the land and into wage work. I.2.3 rules out absentee title, but enclosure can still happen through occupancy:

- **A workplace has no size limit.** A farmer, or a company's workers (P40), may make a whole moor or forest their workplace and refuse entry under II.2.5. Land value tax (P25) is the only brake, and it is not in the text.
- **Customary uses aren't occupancy.** Foraging, collecting firewood, grazing a few animals or walking a path do not make land one's home or workplace, so they are unprotected against whoever first occupies the land.
- **Occupying a commons.** Nothing stops someone occupying a well-used green or riverbank as a workplace and excluding its users. P3's candidate wording protects things kept for shared use, but not open land no one keeps.
- **Enclosure by law.** Law cannot forbid entry unless it breaches a privilege (III.1.1), which guards against enclosure by statute, but a government may still let or sell occupancy of land it manages.

Possible guards, none adopted: a right to roam over land that is no one's home, as in Scotland's Land Reform Act 2003 or the Nordic *allemansrätten*; occupancy of a workplace limited to what is actually worked; or customary uses treated as shared use under P3. Each must avoid the opposite failure in P62.

**Proposed cases:** a walker crossing open hill grazing a shepherd calls their workplace; a newcomer fencing a village green and opening a café there; a forager gathering mushrooms in woodland a timber company works.

### P62. The ills of the commons

The opposite failure. Land no one occupies is open to all, with no one entitled to set rules for it: open access rather than a governed commons. Garrett Hardin's "tragedy" describes this case, overuse because no user bears the cost; Ostrom showed governed commons avoid it, but Wroxia has no commons bodies yet (P3, P31). Three ills to guard against:

1. **Overuse.** Anyone may fish a lake or graze a hillside no one occupies until it is exhausted. Only II.2.2 and harm to animals living there (II.2.1, II.2.3) limit it.
2. **Underinvestment.** Occupancy ends when one stops living or working on the land, so planting an orchard or draining a field gives nothing lasting. People may not improve what they cannot keep, and improvements to unoccupied land invite free riders.
3. **Gridlock.** Where many people jointly occupy a building or field, II.2.5 seems to require every occupant's consent to any entry or use, so one person can veto everything: Michael Heller's "tragedy of the anticommons".

The aim is the narrow path between P61 and this: shared land with rules its users make and can change, without any one person, absentee or occupant, being able to enclose it or block it.

**Proposed cases:** outsiders netting a lake fished by a village until the fish are gone; a joint occupant of a shared courtyard refusing consent to a neighbour's delivery; a farmer leaving an orchard they planted, and a newcomer taking its fruit.

### P63. Allodial title

Allodial title is land owned outright, owing nothing to any superior: no lord, crown or state holding the land above the owner. In English law all land is ultimately held of the Crown; in most US states the state keeps eminent domain and property tax, so even freehold is not fully allodial.

Wroxia's occupancy sits oddly between the two. It is allodial in that no one, including either government, holds land above the occupant: II.2.3 and II.2.5 have no condition for compulsory purchase or eviction. But it is not ownership at all, since it ends when occupancy ends and cannot be sold. And land value tax (P25), if adopted, is a rent owed to the public, which looks like a superior claim. Questions:

- **Is there a radical title?** Does nearlaw, the people, or no one hold land ultimately? It matters for territory (P26), for land no one occupies, and for whether a government can grant anything over land.
- **Compulsory acquisition.** As written no government can take occupied land, even for a road or flood defence. Decide whether that is intended.
- **Transfer.** Occupancy cannot be sold, but nothing stops someone charging to leave ("key money") and handing over to a buyer. Is that a sale in all but name, and does it matter?
- **Foreign powers.** A foreign state occupying land, such as an embassy, makes it farlaw's matter (IV.2.1). Could occupancy become a foothold for a claim of sovereignty?

### P64. People who help you move

Helping someone move house should be safe and simple, but several privileges catch helpers and the person moving:

- **Accidental damage.** A friend who drops a television has damaged a held thing (II.2.6). Consent to handling it is not consent to damage, and II.2.6 has no intent test, so the helper seems to owe restitution. Whether only careless helpers should answer, or none, is open; unpaid helpers who answer for every accident will stop helping.
- **Injured helpers.** A helper who hurts their back on steep stairs may claim the person moving put them at unreasonable risk (II.2.2).
- **The gap between homes.** Occupancy ends when one stops living somewhere (I.2.3) and begins only on living somewhere new. Mid-move, the old home may already be unoccupied and the new one not yet occupied, so someone could settle either first.
- **Things in transit.** Belongings in a helper's van are still held by their owner (I.2.4), but the helper is holding them for someone else, not for their own use, so their position, and their protection if someone else damages the load, is unclear.
- **Moving someone else.** Moving a patient from where they live is limited by II.2.3; with consent that is waived, but moving a patient unable to consent, such as an elderly parent, falls under II.2.8, and helpers need to know they are acting for that patient's sake.

The idea is that helpers are somehow protected. Options include a condition on II.2.6 and II.2.2 for help freely given, so that only harm caused deliberately or recklessly is owed; occupancy continuing for a reasonable time after a move begins; or nothing, leaving it to waiver agreed beforehand. Any protection must not let paid removal firms escape liability by calling themselves helpers.

**Proposed cases:** a friend drops the television while carrying it; a helper is injured on the stairs; a squatter enters the new flat the day before the owner sleeps there; a son moves his mother with dementia to a care home.

### P67. Electing Farlaw's representatives

IV.3.2 has citizens elect Farlaw's representatives by Instrument 5, from citizens who stand as candidates, but Instrument 5 is a stub. Decide the number of representatives, the voting system, terms, constituencies if any, and who runs elections. Elections were chosen for Farlaw because judging diplomacy and defence by results is what they do well (P36).

STAR voting is favoured, scoring from 0 to 5 as Instrument 1 does. STAR itself elects one winner, so it fits either single-seat constituencies or a multi-winner variant: Bloc STAR fills every seat by majority, while Allocated Score (STAR-PR) is proportional.
