# Problems

What is wrong with or missing from the Constitution. Nothing here is decided: a
proposed fix is a starting point. Remarks accumulate under each Problem as
thinking develops. Delete a Problem once a Hearing shows the text handles it;
Git keeps its history.

Article references use the current numbering.

## From the second hearings

### P3. Public things belong to no one

Waits for the redesign in P31. Graffiti on a public underpass came out unclear,
because no one lives or works there, so no one possesses it.

> Revised direction: nearlaw owns little itself. Commons are held by the people
> who use them, organised in local commons bodies that set by-laws for their
> use, as in Elinor Ostrom's studies of self-governed commons. This overlaps
> with P31: commons bodies may be the small, geographic layer, with chosen
> communities as the non-geographic one.

**Discussion (4 November):** the public lift in the Luna pilot belongs here,
with P40, not in a new Problem. Distinguish personal use, shared use and custody
for others. A worker caring for a shared thing must not thereby acquire it
personally, nor must an outside investor acquire a claim to land merely by
financing it. Collective use must not depend on every user being present at
once. Test a lift used by a residents' cooperative with and without damage by an
outsider; separately test whether its members can exclude an existing user or
enclose a previously open commons. These are proposed cases, not settled rules.

**Hearings (5 November):** graffiti on a public underpass used by hundreds daily
still split: Haiku found no breach, since no one occupies or holds the walls;
Luna could not say whether anyone holds them. A visitor smashing a residents'
shared lift was mostly found a breach of II.2.6, treating the residents as
holding it, though one run was unclear. Shared use seems to count as holding
when users are identifiable, and not otherwise.

**Candidate, not applied:** add to Holding: "Things kept for shared use are held
in common by their users. Keeping a thing for others gives no exclusive claim to
it." For shared places that no one occupies, extend II.2.5 to protect "places
kept for shared use from damage or being taken out of that use without their
users' consent". This would protect common use without granting absentee land
title or making an organisation a patient. Who counts as a user, how users
consent together, and how use may change without enclosure remain open under
P31/P40; the shared-place addition is not ready to adopt.

**Living Space (10 November):** parks, roads, and courts are land a government
keeps for public use (V.3 and V.4), so nearlaw has title to them. A rough
sleeper in a park can be moved only by a court, and only if they can take a home
at once; a park keeper carrying a refugee's mother out without a court order was
a breach in both runs (LS18). This answers the former Q8.

## Recurring gaps

These were flagged in almost every one of the 28 second-round rulings, and are
now the main source of noise in hearings.

### P10. The boundary court doesn't exist yet (applied, without its make-up)

Article IV.2.5 leaves open who decides whether a matter is near or far. The
working idea is a court chosen by lot, so neither government appoints it,
applying one test: is there an outside party, or a crossing? Its size, term and
powers are undecided.

**Options:**

1. **A panel chosen by lot.** Around seven citizens on short fixed terms,
   deciding only near-or-far questions, binding on both governments. No
   government appoints anyone, so neither can capture it. The cost is uneven
   expertise and possibly inconsistent rulings, eased by publishing reasons that
   later panels follow.
2. **A joint panel with a lot-chosen chair.** Each government appoints equal
   members, and a citizen chosen by lot holds the deciding vote. More expertise
   and buy-in, but the appointees become each side's advocates, and everything
   rests on one person.
3. **No court, just a default.** Every matter is nearlaw's unless farlaw shows
   an outside party or a crossing, and disputes go to the ordinary courts.
   Minimal and in keeping with farlaw's shorter list, but the ordinary courts
   are nearlaw's, so nearlaw would judge its own case.

**Suggested:** option 1, with option 3's default built in: anything not shown to
be far is near.

**Remarks (2 November):** worried this could reach into every small matter.
Refinement: the court hears only challenges. Each government acts on matters it
believes are its own; only a person affected, an Advocate or the other
government can challenge, within a time limit; and the court decides the
category alone, never the merits.

### P11. No way to stop ongoing harm

Article II.2 now permits necessary and proportionate limits on specified
privileges to defend against imminent death, injury or suffering. That includes
confinement or moving someone from where they live, but only so far and for so
long as necessary. It supplies no general power to detain for later prevention,
punishment or investigation. Article II.2.4 states no condition on leaving
Wroxia. Telling someone to stop is ordinary expression and needs no power, but
how to stop repeated harm outside an imminent threat remains undecided. The
coercive-control and graffiti rulings both noted the former absence of a
preventive remedy.

A former draft proposed this condition on II.2.3: "Confinement, decided in a
judicial case, of someone who carried on causing harm after being told to stop."
It is a starting point, not part of the current constitution or a decision. See
P46 on the judicial case and P48 on a shared home.

**Options:**

1. **Protective detainment.** Confinement allowed only while necessary to
   prevent further harm to others, in humane conditions, reviewed at short
   intervals by citizens chosen by lot, and ending as soon as it is no longer
   necessary. Never a set sentence. Keeps people safe, but "necessary" can
   stretch, which is why review matters.
2. **Keep-away orders first.** Someone who has caused harm can be ordered to
   stay away from particular people or places. Confinement is allowed only if
   they break the order and harm is again imminent. Far less loss of liberty,
   but it depends on the order being respected.
3. **No confinement at all.** Only defensive force in the moment, plus
   withholding public services (P41). The purest no-punishment answer, but it
   leaves repeated violence unchecked.

**Suggested:** option 2 with option 1 as the last resort. This would need an
appropriate condition on Article II.2.3.

**Remarks (2 November):** sceptical of citizens chosen by lot deciding over
someone's life: it risks personal liability and popularity contests. New idea to
explore: each person nominates whom decisions about their life are deferred to,
such as family, friends or coworkers, and that idea may apply elsewhere too.

**Drafting direction (4 November):** settle immediate defence separately from
later prevention. Any defensive condition must expressly cover the privileges it
limits, not assume that II.2.1 overrides II.2.3, II.2.5, II.2.6 or II.2.8. It
must leave the absolute rights untouched. Restraint ends when no longer
necessary against the imminent threat; it confers no power to punish,
investigate or take unrelated possessions. An assertion of danger is not proof
of it, and government still bears the burden under III.2.5.

For later prevention, the preferred starting point is a specific keep-away
measure after an independent hearing, with confinement only as a last resort
against serious further harm, prompt review and release when the justification
ends. Thresholds, evidence, review intervals and who decides remain proposals,
not delegated permission to detain (P38/P46). Removing the harmed person from a
shared home must not be the default (P48).

**Proposed case pairs:** change only whether an attack is still imminent when a
bystander restrains the attacker; whether that bystander also takes an unrelated
wallet; and whether police continue restraint after the danger has ended.
Separately compare the same imminent threat from someone able and unable to
understand others' privileges (P57). T31/T32 test later removal and confinement
without an imminent threat; T33–T38 and T41 test the new defence condition.

**Applied draft (4 November), not yet heard:** the shared Article II.2 condition
replaces the narrower defence condition under II.2.1. It expressly covers
II.2.1/II.2.2/II.2.3/II.2.5/II.2.6/II.2.8, leaves every absolute right
untouched, and excludes protected expression as a ground for intervention.
Articles III.2.9–III.2.10 add independent hearings and prompt review of
involuntary confinement or removal, with the justification owed by whoever
imposed it. T33–T38 and T41–T42 target the defence boundaries; T39–T40 target
process. No new power of later preventive detention is adopted. T31/T32 remain
tests of removal and confinement without an imminent threat.

**Hearings (5 November):** the defence condition holds. T31–T32 and T34–T39
agreed as intended, T40 after the prompt was clarified, and T42 too. T33 split
only because one model judged Ilya's attack rather than Jo's restraint; both
agreed the restraint was permitted. T41 agreed on breach but split on whether
the confinement is forbiddable. What remains open is later prevention, not
immediate defence.

### P12. Recovery of restitution (applied; remaining gaps)

Article III.2.3 now assigns recovery to a public recovery service of the
government whose funds paid. Disputed liability or amounts go to a court under
III.2.9. Taking held things to make restitution is allowed under II.2.6, but
inability to pay and the limits of collection still need work.

**Options:**

1. **The Advocates collect.** The Advocate for the kind of right or privilege
   breached recovers restitution, taking held things to the value owed under the
   condition in II.2.6. Simple, but victims wait, and nothing can be recovered
   from someone with nothing.
2. **A restitution fund.** Nearlaw pays victims at once and recovers the cost
   from whoever caused the harm, over time. Victims are made whole quickly; the
   cost is public money and a risk of carelessness when someone else pays first.
3. **Debt with exclusion.** Unpaid restitution stays owed, and the debtor loses
   excludable public services until it's settled (P41). Gentle, but it can trap
   people who simply can't pay. Working it off can only ever be voluntary, since
   Article II.1.3 forbids compelled work.

**Earlier suggestion:** options 1 and 2 together: the fund pays at once, and the
Advocate recovers from the person responsible.

**Decision (2 November):** accepted. Ready to draft.

**Third hearings:** the fund worked as intended in the wrecked-bicycle case.
Unaddressed: how public funds recover from someone with nothing, given that work
can't be compelled (II.1.3).

**Applied draft (4 November), not yet heard:** the paying government's recovery
service replaces the earlier Advocate-collection proposal. Advocates remain free
to represent patients, including a debtor subjected to unlawful recovery. Public
payment is not postponed until collection succeeds.

**Suggested next steps, not adopted:** ordinary law could provide notice of the
amount and its basis, voluntary instalments based on ability to pay, and court
review before disputed collection. Decide what necessities must be protected
from seizure, how long recovery remains owed, and whether an uncollectable
balance is eventually discharged. Do not recover more than the restitution paid
or add collection charges without a separate lawful basis. Inability to pay must
not become compelled work, confinement or automatic exclusion from services.
Allocation of payment between governments and currencies remains linked to
P34/P35. T52–T53 test the present assignment and limits; no insolvency scheme is
adopted.

**Hearings (5 November):** T53 (no forced work to repay) agreed. T52 agreed that
the recovery service cannot finally decide its own disputed demand; it split
only on `forbiddable`. The insolvency questions above remain.

**Trimmed (5 November), not yet heard:** III.2.3 now states only the principle:
the harmed are paid at once from public funds, and the government that paid
recovers it. A recovery service and court route are matters for law, within
II.1.3 and III.2.9.

## Open choices from drafting

### P17. The Advocates

Article IV.4.4 now makes each Advocate an office, headed by a body of three
volunteers chosen by Instrument 1, one facing reconfirmation each year, with 25
years' maximum service (P49). Not yet heard. The Advocates began as a bureau of
their own; how each office is staffed below its head is open. An Advocate may
bring a claim on behalf of any patient under III.2.8, but other powers need
work: whether they can inspect, intervene, or compel a government to act.

### P18. Conditions on public speech and broadcast

Article II.8.1 has no stated conditions; as a privilege, it may still be waived.
Whether any conditions should apply to equitable public free speech and
broadcast, and what they should be, remains undecided. P60 asks what "equitable"
means.

### P19. Citizenship

Which government grants and records citizenship, on what criteria, and who
handles renunciation. Naturalisation looks like a crossing, which would make it
farlaw's.

Citizen records now also determine the size of the bodies under Instrument 1.
Survey, registration, count dates and corrections remain open; see P49. The
sizing rule does not itself assign citizenship administration to either
government.

**Author's note (9th Nov)**: I'd like to make citizenship "sticky" - that if a
government grants it, revoking it becomes difficult. E.g. each day of
citizenship grants one day grace period, and that children can't have their
citizenship revoked at all (but could upon turning 18, with an 18 year grace
period)

### P22. Officials' duty to act

An inspector who sees harm and does nothing breaches nothing as written. Whether
people acting in a government's name have duties to act, not just limits, is
undecided.

### P23. What counts as held

Land is settled: it is claimed only by title, and anything fixed to it that
cannot be removed intact is part of it (I.2.3–I.2.4). Unclear: cash in a till,
which a worker holds for the shop; bank balances, loyalty points, and other
claims on a bank or scheme; digital accounts, cloud storage, and data; whether
copying takes something when the original remains; whether a sealed parcel is a
communication; and who has a claim to stolen goods a third person bought.

**Direction (4 November):** claims on a bank or scheme do not become held things
by assumption (P51); direct control alone must not let theft confer a claim; and
holding should protect a toothbrush through ordinary absence without turning
every loan into a perpetual income-bearing title. Keep any cryptocurrency case
separate.

**Hearings (5 November):** T16 (bank scam) and a loyalty-points scam both agreed
as breaches of II.2.6, the models treating such claims as held by assumption,
which the text has not decided. Cash taken from a till was a breach in every
run, with Haiku asking whether the worker or the shop holds it.

**Proposed cases:** personal use versus custody for another; a cooperative's
shared tool versus an outside investor's claim; and a deceptive transfer of cash
versus a bank balance.

## Decided in the corpus, not yet in the constitution

These were endorsed in the corpus (archived in wroxia-archive.zip) but never
carried over.

### P26. Territory and conquest

Two rules were firmly endorsed: territory where farlaw holds the monopoly on
violence gains its own nearlaw, and annexed populations get votes in farlaw. On
12 November the author asked about seasteads and settlements beyond Earth; the
first rule would cover a settlement on the Moon or Mars (P77). Whether each new
nearlaw counts its own shares under Part V is open.

### P27. Labour-hours, upheld not enforced

An opt-in economy paid by the hour, which no one is compelled into. It may fit
better as one kind of community under P31 than as a function of either
government.

## Bigger design questions

### P30. Who leads each government

Neither government has a head or a way of choosing one. One idea is a Steward
for nearlaw and a Warden for farlaw, chosen by election, by lot, or a mix.
Article 10 on rendering has since been folded into Positive (I.1.8); law-making
is now IV.3, which leaders could join.

### P31. Chosen communities

Farlaw contains dangerous powers but is small in daily life, so it gives
citizens little plurality. One framing is four relationships: to the world
(farlaw), to the land (nearlaw), to a place (commons, P3), and to one another:
communities people choose to join, which could make consensual rules, run their
own services, economy (P27), and currency, and settle members' disputes, with
exit always open and the universal rights binding them all.

Communities can already have land, since members may pool quotas and have title
together (V.3 and V.4). Commons bodies, holding streets, parks, and water with
by-laws made by those who live there, as in Elinor Ostrom's studies, remain an
idea; nearlaw keeps such land for public use.

**Still open:** whether a community can hold things or breach rights (P40); one
community or several; who judges disputes between members of different
communities; when a child can leave; how commons and communities take part in
law-making (P36); the least a commons must provide; and whether fencing or
grazing land moves wild animals from where they live (II.2.3, Q7).

**Remarks (2 November):** needs very deep thought before anything is drafted.
Two named dangers: small communities turning into the worst kind of homeowners'
association, and raids, where a community is flooded with new members to swing
an outcome.

### P32. Farlaw's revenue

If merchants fund farlaw through customs while every citizen votes for it,
funders and voters are different people. Options include fees on crossings of
people and a far share of land tax, since defence protects land.

### P34. Paying for shared institutions

The Advocates and the boundary court belong to neither government. Which
currency pays them, and who raises it?

### P35. The two currencies, simulated

Tests E1 to E4 describe what the two currencies should do under trade shocks and
money printing. None can pass or fail until there's a simulation to run them in.

**What gives near currency its value? (12 November, from a simulated Reddit
thread)** Tax demand (chartalism: Knapp, Mitchell-Innes, Lerner) is the usual
answer, and E4 assumes it, but nearlaw's taxes create little demand: its skim of
licence rent is netted before payout, so no one need acquire near currency to
pay it, and II.1.3 rules out legal tender. Making licence rent payable in near
currency was considered and withdrawn: it makes money a requirement for taking
resources (the Money belief), ties the currency's value to extraction (a
petro-currency, against the Extraction belief and P82), and makes it swing with
commodity prices (against E4). Open: is near currency meant to be strong, which
needs a tax that bites, or optional, which means accepting that it may stay
small, like a community currency beside barter? Charges for land nearlaw keeps
are the steadiest anchor, but vacant land may be scarce. Cancelling pollution
charges was tried and dropped (P82); pollution is now restitution, not a charge,
so it no longer anchors near currency.

## From the project's starting points

Aims set out at the very beginning, and ideas from early conversation, that the
constitution hasn't yet dealt with.

### P36. How law is made

Articles IV.3.1–IV.3.3 now establish Nearlaw's law-making body by sticky
sortition, Farlaw's elected law-makers and a separate sticky-sortition veto
body. Each decides by Instrument 4: a majority of those voting, with more than
half its seats taking part. Election and legislative procedures remain
unfinished. The founding worry remains relevant: a democracy where every vote
can lose, and the law changes underfoot regardless.

**Options:**

1. **Elected assemblies for each government.** Familiar and plainly legitimate,
   but it leaves the founding complaint untouched: you can vote all your life
   and always lose.
2. **Citizens' assemblies chosen by lot.** Laws are drafted and passed by a
   randomly chosen cross-section of citizens, with a referendum only when enough
   people petition against a law. No parties, no campaigns and no permanent
   losers, but less expertise, and members answer to no one.
3. **Different methods for each government.** Nearlaw's laws made by citizens
   chosen by lot; farlaw led by elected people, since judging diplomacy and
   defence by results is what elections do well, with a lot-chosen body that can
   veto. This mirrors Athens, which chose most officials by lot but elected its
   generals. Fits each job, but it's the most complex.

**Suggested:** option 3. Whatever is chosen, the constitution itself should need
more than ordinary law-making to change (now Part VI).

**Decision (2 November):** for farlaw, elected representatives curtailed by a
veto group chosen by lot, for now. Sortition is welcome only where it clearly
fits, not as a default. Nearlaw's method is still open.

**Idea for bodies chosen by lot (2 November):** "sticky sortition". Empty seats
are filled by lot; each sitting member is scored individually by the public,
from 0 to 5, and keeps their seat with an average of 2.5 or more. A fixed bar
per member, not a ranking, so members aren't rivals. Precedents for parts of it:
Venice's doge elections alternating lot and election; the Ostbelgien Citizens'
Council's staggered rotation; Missouri-plan retention votes for judges, which
rarely remove anyone. Settled for now: scores from 0 to 5 (six levels, within
the range rating-scale research favours, and the scale STAR voting uses),
keeping a seat at an average of 2.5 or more, and a maximum of 25 years in total,
to be whittled down later. Still to settle: a minimum number of ratings before
anyone can be removed. Candidates: farlaw's veto body and the Advocates.

**Discussion (4 November):** consider authorising a legislature to supply
missing procedures, not to decide individual cases or suspend constitutional
limits. Article III.1.4 already permits institutions and ordinary law within
those limits. Sticky sortition would choose office-holders, not enlarge their
powers. Instrument 1 still needs article-supplied parameters before use; no
legislature or selection method is adopted by this discussion. Preserve the
earlier farlaw election/veto choice unless it is expressly replaced.

**Applied draft (4 November), not yet heard:** both sortition bodies use
Instrument 1 with 25 years' maximum service, annual reconfirmation and one third
facing each round. Nearlaw's body targets the cube root of the number of
citizens and Farlaw's veto body half that, each rounded down to a multiple of
three with a minimum of three, and both resize under the Instrument. This
supersedes the earlier fixed-size and exponent-0.3 proposals. P49 holds the
unfinished Instrument mechanics.

**Still open:** how Farlaw's representatives are elected (P67); how proposals
are introduced, published and amended; the time allowed for veto and what
happens if the veto body cannot act; deadlock; and the relationship between
law-making and executive offices (P30). These provisions give no power to
override universal protections or the other government's jurisdiction. They do
not adopt an amendment process (now Part VI). T54–T55 test the basic allocation
only.

**Hearings (5 November):** T54 and T55 agreed that the bodies are lawfully
constituted and that the veto takes effect, but split on outcome, `forbiddable`
and, for T55, jurisdiction. The hearing's questions fit institutional cases
poorly: an official refusing to recognise a body limits no privilege, so
"breach" has no clear meaning. The models also did not agree whether a veto of
farlaw's law is farlaw's matter. Treat these as structural checks rather than
rights cases.

### P37. Opting out

The original aim was a state you can opt out of. Renouncing citizenship is
allowed, but it's unclear what changes: whether a non-citizen living in Wroxia
is still bound by its law, what someone who opts out of everything owes for the
land they live on, and in which currency.

**Hearings (5 November):** one part is settled. Someone who renounces
citizenship but stays and punches a neighbour breaches II.2.1 (one run unclear),
because the universal privileges bind every agent, citizen or not. Land and
currency obligations remain open.

### P38. Due process

Articles III.2.9–III.2.10 now require an independent hearing of disputed breach
claims, a chance to answer the claim and evidence, reasons, one review of the
claim at each party's request by another court, and prompt review of involuntary
confinement or removal. Still open: the general burden and standard of proof,
how evidence is gathered and returned, time limits, and what happens to someone
wrongly accused while a case is open. The new confinement review provision
places the burden of justifying confinement or removal on whoever imposed it,
but grants no power to gather evidence by limiting other privileges. How
repeated reviews of continuing confinement work remains open; III.2.9 does not
grant an endless chain of appeals.

**Third hearings:** in the seized-car case both the detention and holding the
car stood as breaches, as intended. But it shows that, as written, any
investigation that holds a person or a thing is a breach, since no condition
covers gathering evidence.

**Discussion (4 November):** ordinary law can supply hearings, evidence rules,
reasons and review under III.1.4, but cannot create a confinement, search or
seizure exception absent from the relevant privilege. An undecided fact is
different from an undecided rule: a court needs a way to find facts; the
legislature may fill procedural gaps only within the Constitution. Priorities
are an independent decision-maker, a chance to answer the claim, reasons, a
burden of proof, timely review and a remedy for wrongful intervention. Choosing
their exact form remains open.

**Hearings (5 November):** confirmed starkly. Police keep a laptop for a week as
evidence with a court's approval after hearing both sides, and the suspect is
later found to have breached II.2.6. Every run still found the seizure a breach
by nearlaw, since no condition of II.2.6 covers evidence and a court's approval
is not one. As written, a court can decide a case but nothing can be held to
prove it.

### P39. Inheritance and accumulation

Nothing says what happens to money and other things at death; land is settled by
V.4 and V.5. The founding aim was to prevent wealth concentrating rather than
redistribute it afterwards; quotas do this for land, but nothing yet does it for
money.

**Hearings (5 November):** confirmed. A son taking cash left in his late
mother's drawer, with no directions, came out unclear in every run.

**12 November:** III.2.1 now says restitution owed to a patient who has died is
used for those who depended on them, or else for others like them. This settles
restitution, not the rest of a dead person's things.

### P40. Groups as agents

Governments answer as agents, but nothing says whether communities, companies or
other organisations can possess things, make agreements or breach rights. The
early question of mobs, where a crowd seems to act with agency no single member
holds, sits here too.

**Third hearings:** hacking a former employer's cloud storage was unclear in
both hearings, largely over whether a company can hold anything or be harmed.
Its clients' contact lists may still be protected as records about them
(II.2.7).

**Hearings (5 November):** the same hack now agrees as a breach of II.2.7, as
copying records about the clients. Haiku added that the company itself, not
being sentient, holds no privilege. Individuals are protected through records
about them; whether an organisation can hold, occupy or be harmed remains open.

### P41. Exclusion as a sanction

An early idea, from the excludable and non-excludable distinction: the state's
only lever against someone is withholding excludable things it provides, such as
transit or services, never their body, speech or privacy. It may be one answer
to P11 and P16.

### P42. Cars and unreasonable risk

Whether ordinary driving puts pedestrians at unreasonable risk under II.2.2, who
decides, and whether streets as commons (P3) change the answer. The founding
preference for walking, cycling and transit has no foothold yet beyond these.

**Hearings (5 November):** confirmed. Driving at the legal limit along a narrow
shopping street, so that pedestrians must step aside, came out unclear in every
run.

## Added 2 November

### P46. The judicial side

I.1.14 now defines a court as independent of the parties; III.2.9–III.2.10 give
a minimal hearing and review process. Still open: who judges, selection and
funding, the detailed procedure, what orders a court may make, and its
relationship with the boundary court and the Advocates. A former proposal for
later preventive confinement depended on a judicial case (P11); the present
defence condition does not adopt that proposal. This overlaps with P38 on due
process.

**Third hearings:** two questions for the judicial side came up: who declares
that a bribed official has lost their role (III.2.6), and how quickly a joint
occupant who causes harm can be separated (P48).

**Discussion (4 November):** keep making general law distinct from deciding an
individual case, even if both bodies eventually use sortition. A governing
assembly must not be able to authorise its own otherwise unlawful detention.
Define a court in Part I before relying on it in a protective condition, and
specify its independence and review route. Allocation between nearlaw and farlaw
concerns the matter under Article IV.2, not a separate set of universal
protections: domestic location alone does not exclude an outside party, and
being abroad does not confer lawful power under the host country's law.

### P47. Nominated trust

An idea from P11 that reaches well beyond it: each person nominates whom
decisions about them are deferred to, such as family, friends or coworkers.
Possible uses include decisions for someone who can't consent, children adding
nominees as they grow, and restorative conferences.

Remarks: for this to shape how others behave towards you, it has to be easily
knowable who you're dealing with and whom they've nominated, as when knowing
someone's father would step in makes people behave differently. Geography makes
norms legible: in a given place you know how to behave. Functional,
non-geographic arrangements blur those lines, so that legibility needs watching.

Known risks: coercion into a nomination, voided by duress but needing
nominations to be private and revocable; people with no one to nominate, needing
a default such as an Advocate; and concentration, if nominations ever aggregate
into public offices, which turns them into a popularity contest.

### P48. Abuse in a shared home

Article II.2's shared defence condition now permits necessary and proportionate
intervention against imminent death, injury or suffering, including restraint or
removal. It creates no general power to exclude a joint occupant after that
threat ends, even after a judicial case. Someone facing repeated but not
imminent harm may therefore still have to leave unless another lawful measure
stops it. Restitution in kind can pay for somewhere else to live, but does not
itself remove the person causing harm. A first draft gave orders that could move
someone out of their home without evidence, and was withdrawn for that reason.
Later protection still needs work with P11/P38/P46.

**Discussion (4 November):** consider this alongside P38/P46 when deciding what
ordinary law may supply. A legislature can establish a process, but cannot
authorise removal contrary to II.2.3 merely by calling it protective. T31/T32
now test actual removal and confinement separately from Ana's request in T25. A
future protective condition must explicitly change those expectations rather
than quietly treating the old cases as passing.

### P49. Instruments

Instrument 2 now holds the reusable directions-and-custody procedure, applied
only to bodies after death by II.1.4. Relevant cases must include it, not just
the articles. This extends the earlier office-selection-only scope of
Instruments noted below. Possible broader applications remain open under P47;
extraction is not permission for any other kind of custody.

Proposal: operational detail such as voting methods, scales, thresholds and
terms lives in appendices called instruments, keeping the articles short. The
open question is how instruments change. Anything governing how power is won or
kept, such as voting systems and terms, should be as hard to change as the
constitution itself, or only take effect after the next election or selection,
so no one can rewrite the rules for their own benefit. Purely administrative
instruments could change more easily. Amendment is now Part VI.

**Decision (2 November):** every part of every voting system, and constants such
as the 0 to 5 scale, the 2.5 average and the 25-year limit, are enshrined in the
constitution with the same protection as the articles, but kept in an appendix
of instruments that the articles point to. An arbiter judging an ordinary case
reads the articles only; instruments matter only when a case is about how
someone came to hold office.

**Sticky sortition, as understood so far:** at each round every sitting member
faces a retention score vote alone, against a fixed bar rather than other
candidates, so STAR doesn't apply. Anyone averaging below 2.5, reaching 25
years' total service, resigning or dying leaves, and every empty seat is filled
by lot. Settled and drafted as Instrument 1: no one is voted in, only
reconfirmed; rounds are staggered for continuity; empty seats go first to
volunteers who weren't just voted out, then to those who were, highest score
first; no minimum turnout, since keeping the electorate engaged is part of the
job. Still open: term length, the share reconfirmed each round, what record
voters judge members by, and whether to draw volunteers in proportion to the
population's makeup.

**Current application (4 November), not yet heard:** Articles IV.3.1–IV.3.2 now
use Instrument 1. Maximum service is 25 years; rounds are annual and one third
face reconfirmation. The target is the cube root of citizen numbers rounded down
to a multiple of three, minimum three. Growth moves halfway from current size
towards target, rounded down to threes; contraction retires only empty seats, no
more than half the vacancies and no further than target. Remaining vacancies are
filled by lot. This supersedes earlier notes on unset timing, fraction and
filling every vacated seat.

**Applied (8 November), not yet heard:** Instrument 1 now takes _seats_, so each
article sets its own size, and _cohorts_ in place of _reconfirmed fraction_: the
number of seats divided by _cohorts_ face each round, and sizes are multiples of
_cohorts_. Nearlaw's body takes the cube root of citizen numbers, Farlaw's veto
body half that, and each Advocate's head three seats; all use three cohorts.

**Unfinished mechanics, parked:** establish the initial three reconfirmation
groups and resolve ties in time since reconfirmation; allocate new members
across groups after expansion and replacement; handle insufficient volunteers,
members with no scores, and the existing highest-score-first wording alongside
selection by lot. Deliberate stalling below the target through rounding is
accepted, not an allowance carried forward. Citizen survey and registration,
counting citizens abroad or unable to act, the measurement date and how a
disputed count is corrected need work with P19. Decide how changes in the count
reach the next round and how death or resignation between rounds is handled. No
further Instrument rules are adopted here.

**12 November:** Part VI answers how Instruments change: like Parts IV and V, at
an average score of 3.5, with changes to how anyone gains or keeps office taking
effect only after the next round or election.

### P50. Guards against farlaw drifting inward

Carried over from the old "Nearlaw and farlaw" write-up. The early United
States, whose federal government began much like farlaw (war, foreign relations,
foreign trade and naturalisation, funded mostly by customs), grew inward by
three routes: ever-broader readings of its commerce power, a domestic income tax
from 1913, and wartime expansion. Of the guards proposed against those routes,
only some are in the constitution:

- **In:** each force enforces only its own government's law (IV.2.4); matters
  are nearlaw's unless shown to be far (IV.2.5); messages are far only while
  crossing (IV.2.1).
- **Not in:** emergencies never change which matters are near or far; farlaw
  taxes only goods, people and money physically crossing the border, where
  IV.2.3 now says only "matters within its own domain"; and territory farlaw
  comes to hold gains its own nearlaw (P26).
- **A choice to make:** farlaw's scope is currently a test (an outside party, or
  a crossing) rather than a list of powers. A list is easier to police; a test
  follows far work wherever it appears.

**12 November:** farlaw is now framed as Wroxia's relationship to the world:
foreign powers, Wroxia's sea and the air above it, and what crosses the border
(IV.2.1.1). Smoke over land is nearlaw's. A treaty binds only farlaw and never
enlarges its domain (IV.2.6, against _Missouri v. Holland_, 1920; hear D3).
Charging for climate harm at the few points where fossil carbon enters (mines
and ports) was offered and declined: left to ordinary law and votes.

**Clarification (4 November):** the Constitution grants no emergency power to
alter jurisdiction. The proposed emergency sentence would be an express guard
against an expansive future reading, not the repair of an existing emergency
exception. Leave it unapplied; test the existing boundary before adding
redundant text.

The write-up also listed what farlaw would do in practice: diplomacy and
treaties, defence, entry and exit, passports and probably naturalisation,
customs, care of Wroxians abroad, embassies and counter-espionage, and territory
beyond Wroxia. Defence relies on volunteers, since no one may be compelled to
work (II.1.3), which matters most in wartime.

## Added after the third hearings

### P51. Promises and debts

Nothing in the constitution makes a promise or contract binding. In the landlord
case both hearings rightly found Alex has no claim to rent, but for the wrong
general reason: unpaid money is owed under no right or privilege at all. The
same would apply to wages, loans and sales on credit. Either breaking a freely
made promise needs to count as a breach, perhaps through holding (what was
promised is owed), or Wroxia deliberately has no enforceable contracts.

**Direction (4 November):** leave general contractual enforcement open. Do not
introduce it indirectly through bank balances or an expanded definition of
Holding (P23). Any later protection for particular promises must preserve the
rejection of absentee land claims, compelled work and consent obtained by duress
or deception.

**Residents (12 November):** five of the eight Claude residents (R6–R13) were
hurt by unenforceable promises, more than by anything about land.

**Decided (12 November):** consent stands until withdrawn, whatever was promised
for it (I.2.5, from P83), so an unpaid price or rent is not owed as restitution;
the remedy is withdrawing consent. Whether any promise should bind remains open.

### P54. Disclosure, in place of keys and passwords

Encryption is protected only indirectly today: II.1.2 bars compelling "any key
or password", II.2.7 protects confidences, and III.1.1 stops encryption being
forbidden. Unlike a lock, encryption can't be forced; it travels with copies of
the data rather than staying in a place; copying sealed data takes nothing; and
the only way in is the person who withholds the key. So the primal concept is
withholding, of which silence about what you know is the oldest form.

Proposed framing: information has three states. **Withheld** (in your head, or
encrypted) is as private as a thought. **Confided** (disclosed to someone in
particular) stays with them. **Public** (freely disclosed) is free, and copying
it breaches nothing, which is the piracy position.

**Proposed case pairs (4 November):** a recording of a remark freely made at a
public meeting versus the same remark confided privately; separately, a leaked
intimate record versus one freely published by its subject. Do not equate public
availability after a breach with the subject's consent to publication. Keep the
public remark's protection under III.1.3 distinct from the recording question.

> **Disclosure** (noun): making information knowable to another. **Right:** Not
> to be made, by force or deceit, to disclose or give access to anything they
> withhold, nor to have their means of withholding weakened. **Privilege
> (II.2.7, reframed):** What one has disclosed in confidence may not be passed
> on without one's consent.

**Hearings (5 November):** T1 (encryption ban) agreed that the ban has no force.
In T6 one run of each model read "police demand" as mere expression, needing
duress before II.1.2 is breached; II.1.2 says "compelled" without defining it.
Police pressing a suspect's finger to her phone's reader agreed as a breach, but
through II.2.7 (reading her messages), with Haiku asking whether a fingerprint
is a "key". Both gaps support the withholding framing above.

This names no keys or passwords, yet covers passwords, fingerprint unlocks,
thoughts, phishing and laws requiring backdoors. US courts distinguish
surrendering a strongbox key from revealing a safe's combination; the test here
is simply whether something withheld is being taken. Open: whether proving
authorship (digital signatures) belongs here too, as the other half of the same
mathematics.

**Restructure (6 November), not yet heard:** II.1.2 now reads "Not to be
compelled to reveal, or give access to, information they withhold." "Key or
password" and "private communications" are gone; T58–T59 probe biometrics. Hear
with T1 and T6.

**Decided (12 November):** the mind is absolute, things are not, as the US
splits the Fifth Amendment from the Fourth. II.1.2 now reads "Not to be made to
reveal, or to help anyone reach, information they withhold", so no one can be
made to speak or unlock anything; reading a device without its owner's help
falls under II.2.7, a privilege with the defence condition. PH1 (a kidnapper's
phone read to find a missing girl) found a breach of II.1.2 under the old
wording; hear it again with T1, T6, T58, and T59.

### P55. Government secrecy

Governments aren't patients, so as written they can keep nothing private, and
leaking war plans breaches nothing. Proposed layers: (1) disclosing what puts
people at unreasonable risk breaches II.2.2, so law may forbid it; records about
individuals are already protected by II.2.7; (2) an official who discloses what
they were entrusted to keep loses the role, as with corruption under III.2.6;
(3) everything else, including misconduct, waste and embarrassment, may be
disclosed freely, protecting whistleblowers. Secrecy is justified by protecting
people, never by the state's own interest. Links to P40 on organisations.

**Hearings (5 November):** confirmed. A farlaw official giving border-defence
plans to a newspaper split between no breach and unclear. Neither III.2.6 (role
loss requires accepting value) nor II.2.7 (records about patients) reached it,
and II.2.2 was raised only as an unproven risk.

**When many share a secret:** each person entrusted holds it in confidence
separately, and discussing it among themselves isn't disclosure. Only the person
who leaks loses their role; there is no collective sanction. Once leaked, the
information is public, so those who repeat it breach nothing, unless repeating
it puts people at unreasonable risk, which II.2.2 forbids for anyone. Knowing a
secret is never evidence of having leaked it: a leak may be anonymous, snooped
from a device or the result of a hack, even when only one person was entrusted.
So role loss requires proof of the act itself, with the burden on the
organisation, as in III.2.5. Most leaks will therefore be unattributable, and
secrets must be protected mainly by prevention rather than sanctions. Where an
anonymous leak endangers people, the fund in III.2.3 still pays those harmed. A
hack from abroad has an outside party, so it is farlaw's matter. Organisations
can protect themselves without law: giving each person a subtly different copy
(a "canary trap") identifies a leak's source, and splitting a secret
cryptographically so several holders are needed to reveal it (Shamir's secret
sharing, 1979; the "two-person rule") means no one person can leak it.

**12 November:** II.2.7 now says "What is said or written in a government's name
is no one's private communication" (first drafted as a condition, which wrongly
let records about individuals be shared too), so leaking a government memo
breaches nothing, while records about individuals stay protected by their own
privilege, and leaks that endanger people still breach II.2.2: the third layer
above. Hear LEAK1 and LEAK2. Still open: whether an official who discloses what
they were entrusted to keep loses the role, and any public-interest defence for
private leaks, such as the Panama Papers.

**12 November, later:** the second layer is applied too, in III.2.7, with tiered
channels as in the EU Whistleblower Directive (2019): an official who discloses
what they were entrusted to keep loses the role, unless they went only to an
Advocate or a court, or went there first and no Advocate acted within three
months. Losing the role is the most it costs them, unless the disclosure
breaches a right or privilege. II.2.7 also presumes that what anyone acting for
a government says about its business is said in its name, against
private-channel dodges such as ministers' WhatsApp groups. Hear LEAK3, LEAK4,
and WA1.

### P56. Where occupancy ends

Land includes buildings and anything fixed that cannot be removed intact, and is
claimed only by title; anything removable intact stays held (I.2.3–I.2.4,
unheard; hear T23 and T73). Occupancy now only marks where someone lives or
works, which matters for privacy (II.2.7) and for who may take land first when
it comes under Part V (V.5.3). Still open, for these and for where someone lives
(II.2.3): whether occasional use, storage, or intended return counts, and
whether a company can occupy anything (P40).

**Hearings (10 November):** T23, with a company claiming a door it fitted,
agreed in only three of six runs; the dissents found forcing the door a breach
of II.2.5, though no one had title.

### P57. Establishing capacity

I.2.2 presumes a human to have been an agent when acting, unless evidence shows
otherwise. Instrument 3 is a stub for establishing that capacity; it supplies no
assessment procedure, compulsory examination or power to confine. The
presumption itself (formerly P20) passed T56–T57 at the 5 November hearings;
this Problem concerns how agency is established in a case, and when a child
becomes an agent.

Decide what evidence can rebut the presumption, who must produce it, who bears
the ultimate burden and what standard of proof applies. A bare assertion of
incapacity, refusal to cooperate and a diagnosis are not interchangeable with
evidence of inability to understand others' rights and privileges or act on that
understanding. Capacity at the time of the act must be distinguished from
capacity to take part in a later hearing, consent to care or make a particular
decision. Consider fluctuating capacity, intoxication and non-human patients
without silently changing the definition of Agent.

The presumption currently includes children. Decide whether developmental
capacity needs a different starting point or procedure, rather than importing an
undefined adulthood threshold. Assessments should consider relevant conduct and
other evidence without requiring the person's cooperation to establish capacity.
Choose independent assessment, a chance to challenge its evidence, privacy and
review safeguards, and how assessment costs are met. None of those procedures is
adopted by the Instrument stub.

Responsibility and protection remain separate: incapacity does not remove a
patient's entitlements, and a finding of incapacity does not itself permit
confinement, forced work, treatment or disclosure. Existing defence and review
provisions still apply. Cross-references: P11/P38/P46/P47/P48. T56–T57 are
initial boundaries, not coverage of all these choices.

**Hearings (5 November):** T56 agreed. T57 agreed once the prompt told models to
read cases plainly; before that, Luna once refused to accept rebuttal at all,
because Instrument 3 "provides no procedure". Haiku cites the empty Instrument 3
as a doubt in many unrelated cases, so the stub itself adds noise. A
six-year-old deliberately breaking a classmate's toy was found in breach in
three of four runs: the presumption reaches young children unless someone brings
evidence otherwise.

## Added 5 November

### P60. What "equitable" means in II.8.1

Article II.8.1 grants "equitable public free speech and broadcast" but never
says what makes it equitable, and no earlier draft recorded the intent. The word
could mean any of:

1. **Equal shares.** Every citizen gets the same access to public platforms, as
   with equal airtime for candidates. Simple and checkable, but most citizens
   will not use their share, and those who do may want far more.
2. **Neutral allocation.** A government may allocate its platforms by any fair
   method, such as lot, queue or need, but never by viewpoint, party or
   standing. This constrains only the allocator, not outcomes.
3. **A floor.** Every citizen has some real means of reaching the public,
   however small, even if others reach far more.
4. **A ceiling.** No one, however rich or popular, may dominate public
   attention. Closest to the founding worry about money drowning out votes, but
   hardest to square with III.1.1 and III.1.3: limiting one person's reach
   forbids expression that breaches nothing.

Because II.8.1 is positive, it is fulfilled only by action of the government in
whose domain it falls (I.1.8). So a private newspaper refusing a letter cannot
breach it, and "equitable" likely constrains only what governments provide or
allocate: their own broadcasters, public noticeboards and spaces, and perhaps
radio spectrum. Whether governments must provide anything at all, and which one
owes broadcast that crosses the border, are open. "Public free speech" may also
add nothing to III.1.3 unless it means a place or channel to speak in.

The spirit needs stating before the word can be tested: is II.8.1 about a fair
process of allocation (2), or about the shape of public attention (1, 3 or 4)?
See P18 on conditions.

**Proposed cases:** a public broadcaster gives the governing body's members more
airtime than its critics; a town allocates its only noticeboard first come,
first served, and one group fills it daily; a government platform down-ranks a
citizen with a million followers to equalise reach; spectrum auctioned to the
highest bidder; a private newspaper refuses a letter.

### P61. Enclosure

England's enclosures turned shared rights to graze, gather fuel, and cross land
into one owner's exclusive title, pushing commoners off the land and into wage
work. In Wroxia title needs quotas staked, so enclosing a moor needs many
pooled, and land in public use when it first comes under Part V stays with a
government (V.5.3). The author chose a right to roam, now a condition on II.2.5:
any patient may cross land, away from homes and crops, causing no harm
(unheard). Still open:

- **Other customary uses.** Foraging, gathering firewood, or grazing a few
  animals on land someone has title to are unprotected (a resident was told
  picking berries in woods was forbidden), and taking wild things for use off
  the land needs nearlaw's licence (V.7), which may catch a basket of mushrooms.
  Since 12 November, V.7 licenses only what does not renew, so the licence no
  longer does.
- **The roam's edges.** Decided 12 November: a principle, not a list, with no
  fencing rule, as in Scotland's 2003 Act, which bars fences meant to deter
  access. Anyone may cross or rest on land, "keeping a reasonable distance from
  where anyone lives or is working, and causing no harm" (applied; hear T82,
  ROAM1, and ROAM2).

**Proposed cases:** a walker crossing open hill grazing; a forager gathering
mushrooms in woodland a timber company has title to; newcomers pooling to take a
moor walkers have always crossed.

### P62. The ills of the commons

The opposite failure to P61: overuse where no user bears the cost (Garrett
Hardin's "tragedy"), underinvestment where improvers cannot keep what they
improve, and gridlock where any one of many can veto use (Michael Heller's
"tragedy of the anticommons"). Ostrom showed governed commons avoid these. The
aim is shared land with rules its users make and can change, without anyone
being able to enclose it or block it.

**Living Space (10 November):** underinvestment eases, since whoever leaves is
paid for what is fixed (V.6; LS6 agreed), and gridlock eases, since a pool
decides by a majority of what was pooled.

**Vacant land (11 November), unheard:** nearlaw now keeps all vacant land until
taken (V.3 and V.4), so it may charge for or limit its use (IV.2.3.1),
enforceable by taking things (II.2.6), and taking wild things for use off any
land needs its licence (V.7). Still open: whether that prevents overuse, and
whether users rather than nearlaw should set the rules (P3, P31).

**Proposed case:** outsiders netting a lake a village fishes until the fish are
gone.

### P64. People who help you move

Helping someone move house should be safe and simple, but several privileges
catch helpers and the person moving:

- **Accidental damage.** A friend who drops a television has damaged a held
  thing (II.2.6). Consent to handling it is not consent to damage, and II.2.6
  has no intent test, so the helper seems to owe restitution. Whether only
  careless helpers should answer, or none, is open; unpaid helpers who answer
  for every accident will stop helping.
- **Injured helpers.** A helper who hurts their back on steep stairs may claim
  the person moving put them at unreasonable risk (II.2.2).
- **Things in transit.** Belongings in a helper's van are still held by their
  owner (I.2.4), but the helper is holding them for someone else, not for their
  own use, so their position, and their protection if someone else damages the
  load, is unclear.
- **Moving someone else.** Moving a patient from where they live is limited by
  II.2.3; with consent that is waived, but moving a patient unable to consent,
  such as an elderly parent, falls under II.2.8, and helpers need to know they
  are acting for that patient's sake.

The idea is that helpers are somehow protected. Options include a condition on
II.2.6 and II.2.2 for help freely given, so that only harm caused deliberately
or recklessly is owed; occupancy continuing for a reasonable time after a move
begins; or nothing, leaving it to waiver agreed beforehand. Any protection must
not let paid removal firms escape liability by calling themselves helpers.

**Proposed cases:** a friend drops the television while carrying it; a helper is
injured on the stairs; a son moves his mother with dementia to a care home.

**12 November:** II.2.1 now excepts harm that could not practicably be avoided,
from the Veganism belief's "as far as possible and practicable". That eases
accidental injury, but II.2.6 (damaged things) still has no such condition, so
the dropped television is unchanged.

### P73. Letting a location for life

Whoever has title may let on any terms (V.3 and V.4), so whoever wins a wanted
plot by lot can let it at full market rent for as long as they have title,
turning its location into money. Capping rent would need someone to watch rents.

### P77. The sea

Inland water is land (I.2.3), but the sea is not, so no one has title to it,
taking fish from it needs no licence (V.7), and nothing settles who may use it
for moorings, piers, fish farms, wind farms, or the seabed. Whether Wroxia's
waters are near or far (IV.2.1), whether Part V should reach them, and where
Wroxia's border runs at sea are open.

**Decided (12 November):** farlaw governs the use of Wroxia's sea and the air
above it (IV.2.1.1), as Wroxia's relationship to the world: placing platforms,
moorings, and turbines, and flight. People's dealings aboard ships, seasteads,
and stations stay nearlaw's wherever they are, much as a ship carries its flag
state's law (hear SEA1, SEA2, and SHIP1). Space follows the same pattern:
registration, orbits, and treaties are farlaw's, and a settlement beyond Earth
would gain its own nearlaw (P26). Still open: where the border runs at sea.

**12 November, revised:** SHIP1 (a fight in a ferry's galley) went to farlaw and
SEA1 (a turbine on the seabed) to nearlaw, as land, in both runs: "the use of"
did not separate places from people. Following the Roman split of _dominium_
(using things) from _imperium_ (governing people), and the flag-state principle,
IV.2.1.1 now gives farlaw "the sea within Wroxia's border, its bed, and the air
above them, except what people do to one another there", and I.2.3 says land
ends where the sea begins. Hear SEA1, SEA2, and SHIP1 again.

**12 November, again:** with the exception, SHIP1 still went to farlaw in both
runs and SEA2 split, since models read "the sea" as a place and skipped the
exception. IV.2.1.1 now names uses instead: "what is placed in or taken from
Wroxia's sea and its bed, and flight above them". Hear SEA1, SEA2, and SHIP1
again.

### P78. Freed livestock

Animals cannot be held as things (II.1.5), so a herder's cattle cannot be sold
or slaughtered, and may be milked only for the cow's own sake (R9). The herd
grows while the grass does not. Nothing says who cares for animals no one may
own, how their numbers are kept within what the land can feed, or what happens
to the herds, flocks, and farms that exist when land first comes under Part V.

### P80. The changeover

Land "first coming under this Part falls vacant" (V.5.3), so on the day an area
joins Wroxia every piece falls vacant at once. Occupants may take first (V.5.3),
but mortgages, absentee titles, and leases simply end, with nothing owed. A
former owner who paid for a house gets nothing for it, because no one with title
exists to ask a sum under V.6, and "what was last staked" has no referent.
Probably intended, as with the crofters' London landlord (R3), but it is the
most dramatic event in the text and is phrased as a detail. Decide what is owed,
if anything, for buildings and debts at the changeover, and how herds and farms
are treated (P78). Related: P26 on territory.

### P81. Claims across the two governments

Each government's forces enforce only its own law (IV.2.4), and II.2.6 lets
things be taken only for restitution or for nearlaw's charges under IV.2.3.1.
Nothing says how restitution owed under farlaw reaches things held in nearlaw's
domain, or how a farlaw charge is collected at all. Related: P12, P32, P35.

### P82. A government paid by harm

Nearlaw may raise only a skim of licence rent, pollution charges, and charges
for using land it keeps (IV.2.3.1). Its income therefore grows with extraction
and pollution, and a clean, unextractive Wroxia starves it, while it must still
keep enough plots for anyone landless (II.7.1, V.1 and V.3). Decide whether that
incentive is acceptable, or whether nearlaw needs a source of income that does
not scale with harm.

**12 November:** pollution of Wroxia's sea and the air above it is now farlaw's
(IV.2.1.1), so its charges go to farlaw; smoke over land stays nearlaw's.

**Idea (12 November):** nearlaw cancels pollution charges rather than spending
them, as modern monetary theory says taxes effectively are, and as Ethereum
burns its base fees (EIP-1559, 2021). Nearlaw then gains nothing from pollution.
Costs: burning benefits holders in proportion to what they hold, where a
dividend would be equal; restitution to identifiable victims must still go to
them under III.2.1, so only the diffuse remainder is burned; and nearlaw funds
itself more by issuing currency, checked by inflation (E2).

**Decided (12 November):** after two independent second opinions, the burn was
dropped. Both found that cancelling still gives nearlaw room to issue more
without inflation, rewards holders of near currency in proportion to their
holdings, and leaves no one made whole; and diffuse pollution is not harm to no
one, but harm to many patients who cannot be identified. Pollution charges are
gone from IV.2.3.1. III.2.1 now says restitution owed to a patient who has died,
or who cannot be identified, is used for those who depended on them, or else for
others like them; the Advocates bring such claims (III.2.8), and nearlaw pays
out and recovers (III.2.3), netting nothing. Pollution too slight to breach
II.2.2 goes unpriced, though law may forbid polluting kinds of act (III.1.1).
Still open: the licence-rent skim scales with extraction below its cap, and land
charges reward nearlaw for keeping land vacant. Hear BURN1.
