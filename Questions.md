### Q6. A floor for the destitute (R1)

A simulated refugee family with a diabetic grandmother got no shelter, food, or
medicine for three years. Somewhere to live is now answered by land at once
(II.7.1), but III.1.4 still only permits law to provide other services, never
requires it. Add a universal limited positive right, fulfilled by nearlaw: "To
food and urgent medical care, when unable to obtain them." Conditions could
limit it to what nearlaw can practicably supply. Like II.7.1, it would place a
positive duty on nearlaw. **Recommend yes**, since the Preamble puts safety from
harm first and nothing else guarantees survival; but it is a large ideological
choice.

### Q7. Do wild animals occupy where they live? (P31, R2)

I.2.3 says land is occupied by those who live or work there, and no patient may
be moved from where they live (II.2.3). Offline, all runs let a rancher fence
moorland where hares and ground-nesting birds live, and graze cattle there,
without asking whether the animals occupy it. If they do, almost no open land is
free to settle. **Recommend** that an animal's den, nest, or burrow is its home,
but its range is not, so settlers must work around homes rather than avoid whole
habitats.

### Q8. The building sum (V.6.1) (P51)

V.6.1 is the one private debt the constitution enforces: a missed payment ends
the taker's title. Nobody is named to decide whether a payment was missed
(III.2.9 courts hear only claims of breach), in-kind payment is allowed but hard
to prove, and "yearly if they wish" sets no term, so a taker could pay £1 a year
forever. T71 also promises the seller "the rest first" from the next taker,
which the text no longer says. Three ways forward:

- **A. Tidy the private debt:** a court decides missed payments; equal yearly
  parts over at most so many years, or as agreed; restore T71's promise.
  Smallest change, but the state still polices a private debt.
- **B. Nearlaw lends:** Nearlaw pays the seller at once, in near, and the taker
  repays Nearlaw interest-free on a schedule set by law. Nearlaw checks only its
  own records, and repayments give near currency demand; but Nearlaw becomes
  everyone's lender, and defaults fall on the public.
- **C. Lump sum only:** no instalments and no debt, but built plots go to those
  with savings, against the Money belief.

In-kind deals survive under A and B as private arrangements, without the plot as
security. **Recommend B**, but it depends on how near currency is settled (P35).

### Q12. Is cash guaranteed?

The Cash belief has no clause. IV.2.3.1 mentions notes only as the way the
hearth charge is taken, and the yearly exchange is the moment a holder could be
recorded. **Recommend** adding to IV.2.2 that each currency exists as notes
anyone may hold, pass, and exchange unrecorded, then hearing it with T98. A
stamp bought and stuck on the note, as at Wörgl
(essays/2026-11-16-Precedents.md), would take the charge without any exchange.

**Author (16 November):** physical currency ought to be provided on demand, as a
constitutional matter: computers are fallible and the future uncertain. Digital
money should stay lawful. Ready to draft.

### Q13. Is a hearth an hour? (P35)

P35 leaves open whether hearth is denominated in labour hours. **Recommend
yes**: Nearlaw pays one hearth for one hour of any work, to anyone who offers,
and service (V.8) can never be paid off in hearth. The author (16 November)
holds the belief in hourly credit from Cockshott and Cottrell's _Towards a New
Socialism_, without its central planning, and wants travel and training counted
as paid time. Still to choose: one hearth an hour for all work, or that as a
base with more allowed for scarce skills. See
essays/2026-11-16-Hearth-and-haven.md and P89.
