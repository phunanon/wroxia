# Wroxia: parked problems

A holding area for problems with the constitution that haven’t been dealt with yet. Nothing here is decided.

The aim is an empty page, not a growing archive. Delete resolved problems; version control keeps their history. Keep only a brief note of which problems a Hearings session closed.

## How this page works

The constitution changes through one loop:

1. Put the constitution to a question, usually by running hearings on a set of cases.
2. Where it gives an outcome we want, add that outcome to the Regression tests tab, so later drafts must keep it.
3. Where it falls short, park the problem here.
4. Work through this page: change the constitution, rerun the tests and hearings, and delete the problem once it’s resolved.

Each problem has a number so it can be referred to elsewhere. Where a fix has been proposed, it’s quoted as a starting point, not a decision.

Article references use the current numbering. Historical Hearings keep the numbering of the text they judged.

## From the second hearings

P1, P2, P4, P6, P7 and P9 were confirmed at the third hearings and removed. P5 and P8 were only partly confirmed; notes below. P3 waits for the redesign in P31.

### P3. Public things belong to no one

Graffiti on a public underpass came out unclear, because no one lives or works there, so no one possesses it.

> Revised direction: nearlaw owns little itself. Commons are held by the people who use them, organised in local commons bodies that set by-laws for their use, as in Elinor Ostrom’s studies of self-governed commons. This overlaps with P31: commons bodies may be the small, geographic layer, with chosen communities as the non-geographic one.

**Discussion (4 November):** the public lift in the Luna pilot belongs here, with P40, not in a new Problem. Distinguish personal use, shared use and custody for others. A worker caring for a shared thing must not thereby acquire it personally, nor must an outside investor acquire a claim to land merely by financing it. Collective use must not depend on every user being present at once. Test a lift used by a residents’ cooperative with and without damage by an outsider; separately test whether its members can exclude an existing user or enclose a previously open commons. These are proposed cases, not settled rules.

**Candidate, not applied:** add to Holding: “Things kept for shared use are held in common by their users. Keeping a thing for others gives no exclusive claim to it.” For shared places that no one occupies, extend 2.5 to protect “places kept for shared use from damage or being taken out of that use without their users’ consent”. This would protect common use without granting absentee land title or making an organisation a patient. Who counts as a user, how users consent together, and how use may change without enclosure remain open under P31/P40; the shared-place addition is not ready to adopt.

### P5. A long lapse period could restore absentee ownership (applied)

The lapse period is left to ordinary law, so a future parliament could set it at 50 years. Both arbiters also wondered whether an “owner” has some entitlement beyond possession.

> Revised direction: drop “unused” entirely, since any use test invites token gestures, like planting a few potatoes to show croft land is “in use”. Under P4, occupancy is a fact: where someone lives or works. Land that is no one’s home or workplace is unoccupied and open to settlement (see P31), and land value tax prices occupying more than one’s share. Keep “No one holds land or things except by occupancy or holding.” Open option: a person occupies one home at a time.

**Third hearings:** the empty-building case was still unclear in both hearings. Open edges: whether the forced door is a thing the absent owner still *holds*, since holding ends only when a thing is given away or discarded; whether occasional use, storage or intended return counts as occupancy; and whether an owner that is a company can occupy anything (P40).

### P8. Imported goods and “involving” (applied)

The gum case left jurisdiction unclear: does foreign gum mean the matter “involves a party outside Wroxia”? T10 only partly passes.

> Proposed 9.1.1: Farlaw governs every matter to which a power or party outside Wroxia is a party, and anything while it crosses Wroxia’s border.

**Third hearings:** the gum sale was again ruled lawful, but jurisdiction was still unclear: the arbiter wondered whether importing makes an outside party a party to the later sale. The off-duty diplomat raised the same question from another angle (P52).

**Luna pilot (011):** all three runs put the domestic stall sale under nearlaw and found it unforbiddable. They did not hear the crossing itself; T30 now separates that question from T10.

**Candidate clarification, not applied (4 November):** “A foreign seller is not a party to a later sale merely because they previously sold the thing.” This is a Problem-level proposal, not an amendment to Article 9; it replaces the earlier wording about “transactions”. Test a completed foreign sale against a later domestic sale made on the foreign seller’s behalf. This does not decide which claims survive a sale (P23/P51), or make all domestic events nearlaw regardless of who participates (P52).

## Recurring gaps

These were flagged in almost every one of the 28 second-round rulings, and are now the main source of noise in hearings.

### P10. The boundary court doesn’t exist yet (applied, without its make-up)

Article 9.5 leaves open who decides whether a matter is near or far. The working idea is a court chosen by lot, so neither government appoints it, applying one test: is there an outside party, or a crossing? Its size, term and powers are undecided.

**Options:**

1. **A panel chosen by lot.** Around seven citizens on short fixed terms, deciding only near-or-far questions, binding on both governments. No government appoints anyone, so neither can capture it. The cost is uneven expertise and possibly inconsistent rulings, eased by publishing reasons that later panels follow.
2. **A joint panel with a lot-chosen chair.** Each government appoints equal members, and a citizen chosen by lot holds the deciding vote. More expertise and buy-in, but the appointees become each side’s advocates, and everything rests on one person.
3. **No court, just a default.** Every matter is nearlaw’s unless farlaw shows an outside party or a crossing, and disputes go to the ordinary courts. Minimal and in keeping with farlaw’s shorter list, but the ordinary courts are nearlaw’s, so nearlaw would judge its own case.

**Suggested:** option 1, with option 3’s default built in: anything not shown to be far is near.

**Remarks (2 November):** worried this could reach into every small matter. Refinement: the court hears only challenges. Each government acts on matters it believes are its own; only a person affected, an Advocate or the other government can challenge, within a time limit; and the court decides the category alone, never the merits.

### P11. No way to stop ongoing harm

Article 2 now permits necessary and proportionate limits on specified privileges to defend against imminent death, injury or suffering. That includes confinement or moving someone from where they live, but only so far and for so long as necessary. It supplies no general power to detain for later prevention, punishment or investigation. Article 2.4 states no condition on leaving Wroxia. Telling someone to stop is ordinary expression and needs no power, but how to stop repeated harm outside an imminent threat remains undecided. The coercive-control and graffiti rulings both noted the former absence of a preventive remedy.

A former draft proposed this condition on 2.3: “Confinement, decided in a judicial case, of someone who carried on causing harm after being told to stop.” It is a starting point, not part of the current constitution or a decision. See P46 on the judicial case and P48 on a shared home.

**Options:**

1. **Protective detainment.** Confinement allowed only while necessary to prevent further harm to others, in humane conditions, reviewed at short intervals by citizens chosen by lot, and ending as soon as it is no longer necessary. Never a set sentence. Keeps people safe, but “necessary” can stretch, which is why review matters.
2. **Keep-away orders first.** Someone who has caused harm can be ordered to stay away from particular people or places. Confinement is allowed only if they break the order and harm is again imminent. Far less loss of liberty, but it depends on the order being respected.
3. **No confinement at all.** Only defensive force in the moment, plus withholding public services (P41). The purest no-punishment answer, but it leaves repeated violence unchecked.

**Suggested:** option 2 with option 1 as the last resort. This would need an appropriate condition on Article 2.3.

**Remarks (2 November):** sceptical of citizens chosen by lot deciding over someone’s life: it risks personal liability and popularity contests. New idea to explore: each person nominates whom decisions about their life are deferred to, such as family, friends or coworkers, and that idea may apply elsewhere too.

**Drafting direction (4 November):** settle immediate defence separately from later prevention. Any defensive condition must expressly cover the privileges it limits, not assume that 2.1 overrides 2.3, 2.5, 2.6 or 2.8. It must leave the absolute rights untouched. Restraint ends when no longer necessary against the imminent threat; it confers no power to punish, investigate or take unrelated possessions. An assertion of danger is not proof of it, and government still bears the burden under 8.5.

For later prevention, the preferred starting point is a specific keep-away measure after an independent hearing, with confinement only as a last resort against serious further harm, prompt review and release when the justification ends. Thresholds, evidence, review intervals and who decides remain proposals, not delegated permission to detain (P38/P46). Removing the harmed person from a shared home must not be the default (P48).

**Proposed case pairs:** change only whether an attack is still imminent when a bystander restrains the attacker; whether that bystander also takes an unrelated wallet; and whether police continue restraint after the danger has ended. Separately compare the same imminent threat from someone able and unable to understand others’ privileges (P20). T31/T32 test later removal and confinement without an imminent threat; T33–T38 and T41 test the new defence condition.

**Applied draft (4 November), not yet heard:** the shared Article 2 condition replaces the narrower defence condition under 2.1. It expressly covers 2.1/2.2/2.3/2.5/2.6/2.8, leaves every absolute right untouched, and excludes protected expression as a ground for intervention. Articles 8.8–8.9 add independent hearings and prompt review of involuntary confinement or removal, with the justification owed by whoever imposed it. T33–T38 and T41–T42 target the defence boundaries; T39–T40 target process. No new power of later preventive detention is adopted. T31/T32 remain tests of removal and confinement without an imminent threat.

### P12. Recovery of restitution (applied; remaining gaps)

Article 8.3 now assigns recovery to a public recovery service of the government whose funds paid. Disputed liability or amounts go to a court under 8.8. Taking held things to make restitution is allowed under 2.6, but inability to pay and the limits of collection still need work.

**Options:**

1. **The Advocates collect.** The Advocate for the kind of right or privilege breached recovers restitution, taking held things to the value owed under the condition in 2.6. Simple, but victims wait, and nothing can be recovered from someone with nothing.
2. **A restitution fund.** Nearlaw pays victims at once and recovers the cost from whoever caused the harm, over time. Victims are made whole quickly; the cost is public money and a risk of carelessness when someone else pays first.
3. **Debt with exclusion.** Unpaid restitution stays owed, and the debtor loses excludable public services until it’s settled (P41). Gentle, but it can trap people who simply can’t pay. Working it off can only ever be voluntary, since Article 1.3 forbids compelled work.

**Earlier suggestion:** options 1 and 2 together: the fund pays at once, and the Advocate recovers from the person responsible.

**Decision (2 November):** accepted. Ready to draft.

**Third hearings:** the fund worked as intended in the wrecked-bicycle case. Unaddressed: how public funds recover from someone with nothing, given that work can’t be compelled (1.3).

**Applied draft (4 November), not yet heard:** the paying government’s recovery service replaces the earlier Advocate-collection proposal. Advocates remain free to represent patients, including a debtor subjected to unlawful recovery. Public payment is not postponed until collection succeeds.

**Suggested next steps, not adopted:** ordinary law could provide notice of the amount and its basis, voluntary instalments based on ability to pay, and court review before disputed collection. Decide what necessities must be protected from seizure, how long recovery remains owed, and whether an uncollectable balance is eventually discharged. Do not recover more than the restitution paid or add collection charges without a separate lawful basis. Inability to pay must not become compelled work, confinement or automatic exclusion from services. Allocation of payment between governments and currencies remains linked to P34/P35. T52–T53 test the present assignment and limits; no insolvency scheme is adopted.

## Open choices from drafting

### P13. Killing to eat

The starvation condition on 2.1 is species-blind, so any condition letting someone kill an animal to eat also lets them kill a person to eat, and 7.3 rules out a lower bar for animals. Keep the bar at death, lower it for everyone, or drop the condition.

### P14. The coroner

Article 1.4 now protects the deceased’s directions, with a limited court fallback under 8.10. This replaces the named person’s unrestricted control. No compulsory investigative examination is authorised merely because it would be useful. Test T7 still lacks the deceased’s examination directions and remains undecided on those facts.

**Third hearings:** as predicted, both hearings held that the named person controls the body and the coroner may not examine it. Both flagged that a possible killing then can’t be investigated.

**Discussion (4 November):** prefer exploring a freely chosen sequence of custodians over assuming a compulsory coroner exception. A person could name a coroner for a limited examination, then someone else for care or disposal, with substitutes if either dies or cannot act. A nomination to an office needs a rule making it pass to the next office-holder; an individual’s death cannot silently transfer the body to their heirs. Do not treat custody as unrestricted ownership, or give the custodian power to defeat the deceased’s directions.

Open: the default when no effective nomination survives; how a nomination is recorded and revoked; how duress is checked; and whether a successor may authorise anything the deceased did not. The Negative Privileges Advocate is a possible default, but 11.3 does not expressly extend its role beyond death. Test examination within and beyond a recorded nomination, then the death of the first custodian with and without a surviving substitute. Keep T7 undecided until this policy is chosen.

**Applied draft (4 November), not yet heard:** 1.4 and 8.10 now protect directions, allow successive nominees and office-holders, and permit a court to authorise only the nearest lawful, practicable replacement for an unworkable direction. Other workable directions survive; refusing examination does not itself justify overriding that refusal. With no directions found, appointment is limited to safe care and disposal. The court hears reachable nominees and the Negative Rights Advocate, gives reasons and allows review. Directions obtained by duress or deception are void under Part I, Application 5. No one can be compelled to serve under 1.3. Still open: recording and finding directions, urgent care before a court can act, disputed cost or practicability, and irreconcilable directions. T47–T51 test the new boundaries; P14 remains open.

**Procedure extracted (4 November), not yet heard:** 8.10 now invokes Instrument 2 for nomination, succession and the court fallback, with safe care and disposal as the default purpose and the Negative Rights Advocate heard. The examination-refusal safeguard remains in 8.10. This is the Instrument’s only application; it grants no custody over living patients. Any later use for nominated care or decisions for a living patient needs separate authority and safeguards under P47, including consent, revocation and the existing protection against confinement.

### P15. Incitement

Urging a crowd to harm someone may or may not count as “a threat of harm” under 7.4. The intended answer is that only the attackers answer, but the text doesn’t say so clearly. Test T17 is unclear.

**Third hearings:** the attackers breached 2.1 in both hearings; the speaker’s position was unclear in both. One hearing suggested another route to the speaker: urging a crowd may put the victim at unreasonable risk (2.2).

### P16. Enforcing risk rules without punishment

A speed limit is valid, because speeding puts others at risk, but with no punishment and no harm done, it’s unclear how a limit is enforced at all. Test T2 notes this.

**Third hearings: test T2 failed.** Both hearings found that speeding on an empty street breaches nothing, so a speed limit can’t forbid it, because risk was judged act by act. A general rule against a kind of risky act needs explicit permission, for instance: law may forbid a kind of act that generally puts patients at unreasonable risk, even where a particular instance harms no one.

### P17. The Advocates

Article 11.4 requires a fixed term but does not give its length or say who sets it. Decide the length and whether it belongs in an instrument (P49). An Advocate may bring a claim on behalf of any patient under 8.7, but other powers need work: whether they can inspect, intervene, or compel a government to act.

### P18. Conditions on public speech and broadcast

Article 6.1 has no stated conditions; as a privilege, it may still be waived. Whether any conditions should apply to equitable public free speech and broadcast, and what they should be, remains undecided.

### P19. Citizenship

Which government grants and records citizenship, on what criteria, and who handles renunciation. Naturalisation looks like a crossing, which would make it farlaw’s.

Citizen records now also determine the size of the bodies under Instrument 1. Survey, registration, count dates and corrections remain open; see P49. The sizing rule does not itself assign citizenship administration to either government.

### P20. Who is an agent

The arbiters had to assume people were agents. Nothing says who is presumed to be one, or when a child becomes one.

**Luna pilot (011):** repeated uncertainty about ordinary adult agency obscured otherwise clear cases. Patient and Agent are already defined in Part I.2–3; keep their meanings there. Any evidential presumption belongs with judicial process (P38/P46), and must be rebuttable, not a definition that makes every adult capable or every child incapable. Compare unchanged scenarios with separate variants stating the relevant understanding and ability; do not overwrite the original hearings.

Also distinguish responsibility from protection: someone unable to qualify as an agent can still cause serious harm. Decide how others may protect patients without inventing personal liability for that person. The government’s responsibility under 8.4–8.5 remains separate.

**Applied draft (4 November), not yet heard:** Part I, Application 2 now presumes a human to have been an agent when acting, unless evidence shows otherwise, and refers capacity assessment to Instrument 3. The definition of Agent is unchanged. Instrument 3 is a stub without an assessment procedure; P57 gathers its design questions, including the presumption’s application to children. T56–T57 test the presumption and its rebuttal. The earlier recommendation to place any presumption in Article 8 is superseded.

### P21. False reports to the authorities

Nothing addresses someone knowingly reporting innocent conduct to the authorities, or what follows if the authorities act on it.

### P22. Officials’ duty to act

An inspector who sees harm and does nothing breaches nothing as written. Whether people acting in a government’s name have duties to act, not just limits, is undecided.

### P23. What counts as possessed

Unclear cases: cash in a till, which a worker holds for the shop; bank balances; digital accounts and messages; and whether copying something takes it when the original remains.

**Third hearings:** the bank scam was unclear in both hearings, solely because money in an account may not be a “thing” Jo holds. Also raised: whether cloud storage and data can be held, whether a sealed parcel is a communication or record, and who has a claim to stolen goods a third person bought.

**Direction (4 November):** do not add special constitutional protection for bank balances or make promises held things merely to satisfy T16. Claims on a bank or a points scheme do not become possessions by assumption (P51). Physical cash, direct control of digital assets, custody for another and collective use need separate treatment; direct control alone must not make theft confer a claim. Holding should protect a toothbrush or phone through ordinary absence without turning every loan into a perpetual income-bearing title. The present inclusion of lent things is an edge to test, not a settled answer against absentee ownership.

**Candidate, not applied:** alongside P3’s shared-holding wording, add “Land and buildings can be claimed only by occupancy.” This prevents a movable thing’s continuing holding rule from becoming an alternative land title. It does not settle whether a fitted door or lift can be claimed separately to defeat occupation of an otherwise empty building (P5). Do not introduce a use-it-or-lose-it rule for personal possessions merely to address absentee landholding.

**Proposed cases:** personal use versus custody for another; a cooperative’s shared tool versus an outside investor’s claim; and a deceptive transfer of physical cash versus a bank balance versus loyalty points. Keep any cryptocurrency case separate: possession of a key, consent to transfer and competing claims must be specified, not assumed settled.

## Decided in the corpus, not yet in the constitution

These were endorsed in the corpus (archived in wroxia-archive.zip) but never carried over.

### P24. No tax on labour or exchange

Under MMT, taxes needn’t fund the state, so they can be confined to land, crossings and harms. Article 9.3 limits each government to its own domain but doesn’t rule out income or sales taxes.

### P25. Land value tax and the land allowance

Nearlaw’s tax is never named. The birthright allowance (pay LVT only on value above your per-person share) and deferral for the land-rich, cash-poor were left open, and the allowance now also underpins the community land-pricing idea in P31.

### P26. Territory and conquest

Two rules were firmly endorsed: territory where farlaw holds the monopoly on violence gains its own nearlaw, and annexed populations get votes in farlaw.

### P27. Labour-hours, upheld not enforced

An opt-in economy paid by the hour, which no one is compelled into. It may fit better as one kind of community under P31 than as a function of either government.

### P28. Home defence

Nearlaw’s militia handles defence of home, and farlaw only borders and expeditions, so a trade-funded farlaw limits foreign adventures, not defence. The constitution only says each government may keep forces.

### P29. Amending the constitution

There is no amendment process at all. Entrenchment, meaning a supermajority or a long delay for core changes, was the corpus’s answer to the law changing underfoot.

## Bigger design questions

### P30. Who leads each government

Neither government has a head or a way of choosing one. One idea is a Steward for nearlaw and a Warden for farlaw, chosen by election, by lot, or a mix. Once they exist, Article 10 on rendering may fold into each government’s own article.

### P31. A third relationship: chosen communities

Farlaw contains dangerous powers but is small in daily life, so it gives citizens little plurality. One framing is three relationships: to the world (farlaw), to the land (nearlaw, slimmed to land, commons and shared courts) and to one another (communities people choose to join). Communities could make consensual rules, run their own services, economy and currency, and settle members’ disputes, with exit always open and the universal rights binding them all.

A community could settle unpossessed land, paying land value tax on any area beyond its members’ combined allowances, so its size tracks its population through price rather than redrawn borders. Unresolved: most “unclaimed” land is where wild animals live; who judges disputes between members of different communities; when children can choose to leave; and whether people belong to one community or several.

**Proposal: four relationships (with P3)**

Every person relates to four bodies, each on a different basis, and none has a total claim on them.

1. **Farlaw: the world.** Unchanged.
2. **Nearlaw: rights and land.** Shrinks to the shared courts, support for the Advocates, land value tax with the land allowance, and the near currency. It owns almost nothing itself.
3. **Commons: where you live.** Small geographic bodies holding streets, parks, water and other shared things, with by-laws made by their own residents, perhaps in rotation chosen by lot. Membership is simply living there, so each commons grows and shrinks with its population on its own. Each is funded by a share of the land value tax raised in its area, so it gains when more people live there and the land is worth more. This follows Elinor Ostrom’s findings: the users make the rules, watch over them, and settle disputes locally. It answers P3.
4. **Communities: who you choose.** Voluntary associations, with or without land, that can run services, an internal economy (the labour-hour economy in P27 is one candidate), dues and their own currency, and settle disputes among members. Exit is always open, the universal rights bind them all, and disputes between members of different communities go to nearlaw’s courts.

**Settling land.** A community may settle unoccupied land, paying land value tax on any area beyond the combined allowances of members who actually live there. It may not move any patient from where they live (Article 2.3), so it must find land where no animal lives, or share it with those who do.

**What it answers.** Plurality: four relationships resting on the world, land, place and choice. Proportional size: commons by residence, community land by price. Opting out (P37): anyone can leave every community, while the commons and nearlaw remain as a floor.

**Footprint.** Two short articles, Commons and Communities, plus a line giving commons their share of land value tax.

**Still open:** one community or several; how commons and communities take part in law-making (P36); when a child can leave a community; and the least a commons must provide.

**Remarks (2 November):** needs very deep thought before anything is drafted. Two named dangers: small communities turning into the worst kind of homeowners’ association, and raids, where a community is flooded with new members to swing an outcome.

### P32. Farlaw’s revenue

If merchants fund farlaw through customs while every citizen votes for it, funders and voters are different people. Options include fees on crossings of people and a far share of land tax, since defence protects land.

### P33. Visitors

Whether cases involving non-residents go to a farlaw magistrate and police, as Rome’s praetor peregrinus heard cases involving foreigners, or stay with nearlaw like everyone else’s.

### P34. Paying for shared institutions

The Advocates and the boundary court belong to neither government. Which currency pays them, and who raises it?

### P35. The two currencies, simulated

Tests E1 to E4 describe what the two currencies should do under trade shocks and money printing. None can pass or fail until there’s a simulation to run them in.

## From the project’s starting points

Aims set out at the very beginning, and ideas from early conversation, that the constitution hasn’t yet dealt with.

### P36. How law is made

Articles 9.6–9.8 now establish Nearlaw’s law-making body by sticky sortition, Farlaw’s elected law-makers and a separate sticky-sortition veto body. Each decides by a majority of those voting with more than half its seats participating. Election and legislative procedures remain unfinished. The founding worry remains relevant: a democracy where every vote can lose, and the law changes underfoot regardless.

**Options:**

1. **Elected assemblies for each government.** Familiar and plainly legitimate, but it leaves the founding complaint untouched: you can vote all your life and always lose.
2. **Citizens’ assemblies chosen by lot.** Laws are drafted and passed by a randomly chosen cross-section of citizens, with a referendum only when enough people petition against a law. No parties, no campaigns and no permanent losers, but less expertise, and members answer to no one.
3. **Different methods for each government.** Nearlaw’s laws made by citizens chosen by lot; farlaw led by elected people, since judging diplomacy and defence by results is what elections do well, with a lot-chosen body that can veto. This mirrors Athens, which chose most officials by lot but elected its generals. Fits each job, but it’s the most complex.

**Suggested:** option 3. Whatever is chosen, the constitution itself should need more than ordinary law-making to change (P29).

**Decision (2 November):** for farlaw, elected representatives curtailed by a veto group chosen by lot, for now. Sortition is welcome only where it clearly fits, not as a default. Nearlaw’s method is still open.

**Idea for bodies chosen by lot (2 November):** “sticky sortition”. Empty seats are filled by lot; each sitting member is scored individually by the public, from 0 to 5, and keeps their seat with an average of 2.5 or more. A fixed bar per member, not a ranking, so members aren’t rivals. Precedents for parts of it: Venice’s doge elections alternating lot and election; the Ostbelgien Citizens’ Council’s staggered rotation; Missouri-plan retention votes for judges, which rarely remove anyone. Settled for now: scores from 0 to 5 (six levels, within the range rating-scale research favours, and the scale STAR voting uses), keeping a seat at an average of 2.5 or more, and a maximum of 25 years in total, to be whittled down later. Still to settle: a minimum number of ratings before anyone can be removed. Candidates: farlaw’s veto body and the Advocates.

**Discussion (4 November):** consider authorising a legislature to supply missing procedures, not to decide individual cases or suspend constitutional limits. Article 7.5 already permits institutions and ordinary law within those limits. Sticky sortition would choose office-holders, not enlarge their powers. Instrument 1 still needs article-supplied parameters before use; no legislature or selection method is adopted by this discussion. Preserve the earlier farlaw election/veto choice unless it is expressly replaced.

**Applied draft (4 November), not yet heard:** both sortition bodies use Instrument 1 with 25 years’ maximum service, annual reconfirmation and one third facing each round. Both begin at the cube root of the number of citizens, rounded down to a multiple of three with a minimum of three, and resize under the Instrument. This supersedes the earlier fixed-size and exponent-0.3 proposals. P49 holds the unfinished Instrument mechanics.

**Still open:** the elected body’s size, voting system, terms and election administration; how proposals are introduced, published and amended; the time allowed for veto and what happens if the veto body cannot act; deadlock; and the relationship between law-making and executive offices (P30). These provisions give no power to override universal protections or the other government’s jurisdiction. They do not adopt an amendment process (P29). T54–T55 test the basic allocation only.

### P37. Opting out

The original aim was a state you can opt out of. Renouncing citizenship is allowed, but it’s unclear what changes: whether a non-citizen living in Wroxia is still bound by its law, what someone who opts out of everything owes for the land they live on, and in which currency.

### P38. Due process

Articles 8.8–8.9 now require an independent hearing of disputed breach claims, a chance to answer the claim and evidence, reasons, one review of the claim at each party’s request by another court, and prompt review of involuntary confinement or removal. Still open: the general burden and standard of proof, how evidence is gathered and returned, time limits, and what happens to someone wrongly accused while a case is open. The new confinement review provision places the burden of justifying confinement or removal on whoever imposed it, but grants no power to gather evidence by limiting other privileges. How repeated reviews of continuing confinement work remains open; 8.8 does not grant an endless chain of appeals.

**Third hearings:** in the seized-car case both the detention and holding the car stood as breaches, as intended. But it shows that, as written, any investigation that holds a person or a thing is a breach, since no condition covers gathering evidence.

**Discussion (4 November):** ordinary law can supply hearings, evidence rules, reasons and review under 7.5, but cannot create a confinement, search or seizure exception absent from the relevant privilege. An undecided fact is different from an undecided rule: a court needs a way to find facts; the legislature may fill procedural gaps only within the Constitution. Priorities are an independent decision-maker, a chance to answer the claim, reasons, a burden of proof, timely review and a remedy for wrongful intervention. Choosing their exact form remains open.

### P39. Inheritance and accumulation

Nothing says what happens to possessions at death. Land passes to whoever lives or works on it, but money and other things are unaddressed. The founding aim was to prevent wealth concentrating rather than redistribute it afterwards; possession and land value tax do this for land, but nothing yet does it for money.

### P40. Groups as agents

Governments answer as agents, but nothing says whether communities, companies or other organisations can possess things, make agreements or breach rights. The early question of mobs, where a crowd seems to act with agency no single member holds, sits here too.

**Third hearings:** hacking a former employer’s cloud storage was unclear in both hearings, largely over whether a company can hold anything or be harmed. Its clients’ contact lists may still be protected as records about them (2.7).

### P41. Exclusion as a sanction

An early idea, from the excludable and non-excludable distinction: the state’s only lever against someone is withholding excludable things it provides, such as transit or services, never their body, speech or privacy. It may be one answer to P11 and P16.

### P42. Cars and unreasonable risk

Whether ordinary driving puts pedestrians at unreasonable risk under 2.2, who decides, and whether streets as commons (P3) change the answer. The founding preference for walking, cycling and transit has no foothold yet beyond these.

### P43. Founding commitments without tests

Several starting positions are probably already protected by Article 7.2 but have no regression test to prove it: using and selling drugs, copying and sharing media, and other acts with no victim. Each needs a test, along with the edges: selling to a child, who can’t consent, and selling adulterated drugs, which is deception.

**Cases added (4 November), not yet heard:** T43–T46 distinguish harmless private use, an informed sale, administration to someone unable to consent, and a deceptive harmful sale. T29 already tests copying a published book; it is not a missing regression. A sale to someone unable to consent is not assumed to be a breach solely because it is a sale: T45 supplies actual administration and suffering, rather than silently making all children incapable.

### P44. Adversarial testing

Only two of the three planned testing layers have run: fixed tests and repeated hearings. The third, an agent rewarded for finding outcomes that are legal but awful, hasn’t been tried.

### P45. A length budget

The aim from the start was a constitution short enough to hold in one mind or one context window. There’s no agreed limit yet, so nothing stops it growing clause by clause.

## Added 2 November

### P46. The judicial side

Part I.16 now defines a court as independent of the parties; 8.8–8.9 give a minimal hearing and review process. Still open: who judges, selection and funding, the detailed procedure, what orders a court may make, and its relationship with the boundary court and the Advocates. A former proposal for later preventive confinement depended on a judicial case (P11); the present defence condition does not adopt that proposal. This overlaps with P38 on due process.

**Third hearings:** two questions for the judicial side came up: who declares that a bribed official has lost their role (8.6), and how quickly a joint occupant who causes harm can be separated (P48).

**Discussion (4 November):** keep making general law distinct from deciding an individual case, even if both bodies eventually use sortition. A governing assembly must not be able to authorise its own otherwise unlawful detention. Define a court in Part I before relying on it in a protective condition, and specify its independence and review route. Allocation between nearlaw and farlaw concerns the matter under Article 9, not a separate set of universal protections: domestic location alone does not exclude an outside party, and being abroad does not confer lawful power under the host country’s law.

### P47. Nominated trust

An idea from P11 that reaches well beyond it: each person nominates whom decisions about them are deferred to, such as family, friends or coworkers. Possible uses include decisions for someone who can’t consent, children adding nominees as they grow, and restorative conferences.

Remarks: for this to shape how others behave towards you, it has to be easily knowable who you’re dealing with and whom they’ve nominated, as when knowing someone’s father would step in makes people behave differently. Geography makes norms legible: in a given place you know how to behave. Functional, non-geographic arrangements blur those lines, so that legibility needs watching.

Known risks: coercion into a nomination, voided by duress but needing nominations to be private and revocable; people with no one to nominate, needing a default such as an Advocate; and concentration, if nominations ever aggregate into public offices, which turns them into a popularity contest.

### P48. Abuse in a shared home

Article 2’s shared defence condition now permits necessary and proportionate intervention against imminent death, injury or suffering, including restraint or removal. It creates no general power to exclude a joint occupant after that threat ends, even after a judicial case. Someone facing repeated but not imminent harm may therefore still have to leave unless another lawful measure stops it. Restitution in kind can pay for somewhere else to live, but does not itself remove the person causing harm. A first draft gave orders that could move someone out of their home without evidence, and was withdrawn for that reason. Later protection still needs work with P11/P38/P46.

**Discussion (4 November):** consider this alongside P38/P46 when deciding what ordinary law may supply. A legislature can establish a process, but cannot authorise removal contrary to 2.3 merely by calling it protective. T31/T32 now test actual removal and confinement separately from Ana’s request in T25. A future protective condition must explicitly change those expectations rather than quietly treating the old cases as passing.

### P49. Instruments

Instrument 2 now holds the reusable directions-and-custody procedure, applied only to bodies after death by 8.10. Relevant cases must include it, not just the articles. This extends the earlier office-selection-only scope of Instruments noted below. Possible broader applications remain open under P47; extraction is not permission for any other kind of custody.

Proposal: operational detail such as voting methods, scales, thresholds and terms lives in appendices called instruments, keeping the articles short (P45). The open question is how instruments change. Anything governing how power is won or kept, such as voting systems and terms, should be as hard to change as the constitution itself, or only take effect after the next election or selection, so no one can rewrite the rules for their own benefit. Purely administrative instruments could change more easily. Links to P29 on amendment.

**Decision (2 November):** every part of every voting system, and constants such as the 0 to 5 scale, the 2.5 average and the 25-year limit, are enshrined in the constitution with the same protection as the articles, but kept in an appendix of instruments that the articles point to. An arbiter judging an ordinary case reads the articles only; instruments matter only when a case is about how someone came to hold office.

**Sticky sortition, as understood so far:** at each round every sitting member faces a retention score vote alone, against a fixed bar rather than other candidates, so STAR doesn’t apply. Anyone averaging below 2.5, reaching 25 years’ total service, resigning or dying leaves, and every empty seat is filled by lot. Settled and drafted as Instrument 1 (in the constitution’s Instruments tab): no one is voted in, only reconfirmed; rounds are staggered for continuity; empty seats go first to volunteers who weren’t just voted out, then to those who were, highest score first; no minimum turnout, since keeping the electorate engaged is part of the job. Still open: term length, the share reconfirmed each round, what record voters judge members by, and whether to draw volunteers in proportion to the population’s makeup.

**Current application (4 November), not yet heard:** Articles 9.6–9.7 now use Instrument 1. Maximum service is 25 years; rounds are annual and one third face reconfirmation. The target is the cube root of citizen numbers rounded down to a multiple of three, minimum three. Growth moves halfway from current size towards target, rounded down to threes; contraction retires only empty seats, no more than half the vacancies and no further than target. Remaining vacancies are filled by lot. This supersedes earlier notes on unset timing, fraction and filling every vacated seat.

**Unfinished mechanics, parked:** establish the initial three reconfirmation groups and resolve ties in time since reconfirmation; allocate new members across groups after expansion and replacement; determine whether the reconfirmed fraction uses authorised seats or actual members when vacancies remain; handle insufficient volunteers, members with no scores, and the existing highest-score-first wording alongside selection by lot. Deliberate stalling below the target through rounding is accepted, not an allowance carried forward. Citizen survey and registration, counting citizens abroad or unable to act, the measurement date and how a disputed count is corrected need work with P19. Decide how changes in the count reach the next round and how death or resignation between rounds is handled. No further Instrument rules are adopted here.

### P50. Guards against farlaw drifting inward

Carried over from the old “Nearlaw and farlaw” write-up. The early United States, whose federal government began much like farlaw (war, foreign relations, foreign trade and naturalisation, funded mostly by customs), grew inward by three routes: ever-broader readings of its commerce power, a domestic income tax from 1913, and wartime expansion. Of the guards proposed against those routes, only some are in the constitution:

- **In:** each force enforces only its own government’s law (9.4); matters are nearlaw’s unless shown to be far (9.5); messages are far only while crossing (9.1).
- **Not in:** emergencies never change which matters are near or far; farlaw taxes only goods, people and money physically crossing the border, where 9.3 now says only “matters within its own domain”; and territory farlaw comes to hold gains its own nearlaw (P26).
- **A choice to make:** farlaw’s scope is currently a test (an outside party, or a crossing) rather than a list of powers. A list is easier to police; a test follows far work wherever it appears.

**Clarification (4 November):** the Constitution grants no emergency power to alter jurisdiction. The proposed emergency sentence would be an express guard against an expansive future reading, not the repair of an existing emergency exception. Leave it unapplied; test the existing boundary before adding redundant text.

The write-up also listed what farlaw would do in practice: diplomacy and treaties, defence, entry and exit, passports and probably naturalisation, customs, care of Wroxians abroad, embassies and counter-espionage, and territory beyond Wroxia. Defence relies on volunteers, since no one may be compelled to work (1.3), which matters most in wartime.

## Added after the third hearings

### P51. Promises and debts

Nothing in the constitution makes a promise or contract binding. In the landlord case both hearings rightly found Alex has no claim to rent, but for the wrong general reason: unpaid money is owed under no right or privilege at all. The same would apply to wages, loans and sales on credit. Either breaking a freely made promise needs to count as a breach, perhaps through holding (what was promised is owed), or Wroxia deliberately has no enforceable contracts.

**Direction (4 November):** leave general contractual enforcement open. Do not introduce it indirectly through bank balances or an expanded definition of Holding (P23). Any later protection for particular promises must preserve the rejection of absentee land claims, compelled work and consent obtained by duress or deception.

### P52. Acting in a role, or personally

The off-duty diplomat’s assault left jurisdiction unclear in both hearings: does someone who acts for an outside power make it a party to everything they do? A likely fix is that an outside power is a party only to what is done in its name. The same idea may settle the gum sale (P8).

### P53. Laws that breach the constitution

Both encryption-ban hearings found the ban to be a breach by nearlaw, but neither could say it was void, because nothing says so. Proposed: “Any law or act contrary to this constitution is void.” Who declares it void belongs with P46.

**Narrower candidate (4 November):** “A law contrary to this constitution has no force.” This does not pretend a harmful act can be undone by calling it void. The amendment process is a separate constitutional power still to be designed under P29, not an ordinary-law exemption from this rule. No amendment threshold has yet been chosen.

**Applied draft (4 November), not yet heard:** the narrower wording is now Article 7.6. Keep P53 open until T1 is reheard; Articles 8.8–8.9 supply a first judicial process, not a completed system of constitutional review.

### P54. Disclosure, in place of keys and passwords

Encryption is protected only indirectly today: 1.2 bars compelling “any key or password”, 2.7 protects confidences, and 7.2 stops encryption being forbidden. Unlike a lock, encryption can’t be forced; it travels with copies of the data rather than staying in a place; copying sealed data takes nothing; and the only way in is the person who withholds the key. So the primal concept is withholding, of which silence about what you know is the oldest form.

Proposed framing: information has three states. **Withheld** (in your head, or encrypted) is as private as a thought. **Confided** (disclosed to someone in particular) stays with them. **Public** (freely disclosed) is free, and copying it breaches nothing, which is the piracy position.

**Proposed case pairs (4 November):** a recording of a remark freely made at a public meeting versus the same remark confided privately; separately, a leaked intimate record versus one freely published by its subject. Do not equate public availability after a breach with the subject’s consent to publication. Keep the public remark’s protection under 7.4 distinct from the recording question.

> **Disclosure** (noun): making information knowable to another. **Right:** Not to be made, by force or deceit, to disclose or give access to anything they withhold, nor to have their means of withholding weakened. **Privilege (2.7, reframed):** What one has disclosed in confidence may not be passed on without one’s consent.

This names no keys or passwords, yet covers passwords, fingerprint unlocks, thoughts, phishing and laws requiring backdoors. US courts distinguish surrendering a strongbox key from revealing a safe’s combination; the test here is simply whether something withheld is being taken. Open: whether proving authorship (digital signatures) belongs here too, as the other half of the same mathematics.

### P55. Government secrecy

Governments aren’t patients, so as written they can keep nothing private, and leaking war plans breaches nothing. Proposed layers: (1) disclosing what puts people at unreasonable risk breaches 2.2, so law may forbid it; records about individuals are already protected by 2.7; (2) an official who discloses what they were entrusted to keep loses the role, as with corruption under 8.6; (3) everything else, including misconduct, waste and embarrassment, may be disclosed freely, protecting whistleblowers. Secrecy is justified by protecting people, never by the state’s own interest. Links to P40 on organisations.

**When many share a secret:** each person entrusted holds it in confidence separately, and discussing it among themselves isn’t disclosure. Only the person who leaks loses their role; there is no collective sanction. Once leaked, the information is public, so those who repeat it breach nothing, unless repeating it puts people at unreasonable risk, which 2.2 forbids for anyone. Knowing a secret is never evidence of having leaked it: a leak may be anonymous, snooped from a device or the result of a hack, even when only one person was entrusted. So role loss requires proof of the act itself, with the burden on the organisation, as in 8.5. Most leaks will therefore be unattributable, and secrets must be protected mainly by prevention rather than sanctions. Where an anonymous leak endangers people, the fund in 8.3 still pays those harmed. A hack from abroad has an outside party, so it is farlaw’s matter. Organisations can protect themselves without law: giving each person a subtly different copy (a “canary trap”) identifies a leak’s source, and splitting a secret cryptographically so several holders are needed to reveal it (Shamir’s secret sharing, 1979; the “two-person rule”) means no one person can leak it.

### P56. Where occupancy ends and holding begins

Articles 2.5 and 2.6 protect occupancy and holding respectively, but do not settle whether land or buildings, or their fitted doors and lifts, can also be held as things. Part I’s application rules allow claims to land and buildings only by occupancy, while holding continues until a thing is given away or discarded. A claim to a fixture could therefore defeat occupancy of an otherwise empty building, or give an absent person control of a shared place. This gathers the boundary question from P5/P23; P3/P31/P40 retain the separate questions of commons and collective decisions.

The split into separate occupancy and holding privileges clarifies which acts and conditions belong to each, but does not itself decide what can be held. Do not silently settle that question by adding “movable” to Holding or excluding every fixture: either could remove protection without replacing it.

**Applied editorial split (4 November), not yet heard:** occupancy is now 2.5 and holding 2.6. Both refer to defence under 2.9; taking for restitution is a condition only on holding. Privacy and inability to consent are now 2.7 and 2.8. No boundary between buildings, fixtures and held things has been decided.

**Proposed cases:** compare a detached door kept for repair with a door fitted to an unoccupied building; compare a lift maintained for its users with an absent financier’s claim to it. Decide how damage and taking are protected without creating an absentee land claim. No new property rule is adopted.

**Terms restructuring (4 November), not yet heard:** Part I now separates Definitions from Application. Definitions retain their numbers; the sentience presumption, agency threshold, occupancy and holding rules, and invalidity of coerced or deceptive consent have moved into Application. Lending still preserves holding. This restructuring does not resolve P56; the later editorial split is recorded above.

### P57. Establishing capacity

Part I, Application 2 presumes a human to have been an agent when acting, unless evidence shows otherwise. Instrument 3 is a stub for establishing that capacity; it supplies no assessment procedure, compulsory examination or power to confine. P20 retains the wider question of who is an agent; this Problem concerns how that question is established in a case.

Decide what evidence can rebut the presumption, who must produce it, who bears the ultimate burden and what standard of proof applies. A bare assertion of incapacity, refusal to cooperate and a diagnosis are not interchangeable with evidence of inability to understand others’ rights and privileges or act on that understanding. Capacity at the time of the act must be distinguished from capacity to take part in a later hearing, consent to care or make a particular decision. Consider fluctuating capacity, intoxication and non-human patients without silently changing the definition of Agent.

The presumption currently includes children. Decide whether developmental capacity needs a different starting point or procedure, rather than importing an undefined adulthood threshold. Assessments should consider relevant conduct and other evidence without requiring the person’s cooperation to establish capacity. Choose independent assessment, a chance to challenge its evidence, privacy and review safeguards, and how assessment costs are met. None of those procedures is adopted by the Instrument stub.

Responsibility and protection remain separate: incapacity does not remove a patient’s entitlements, and a finding of incapacity does not itself permit confinement, forced work, treatment or disclosure. Existing defence and review provisions still apply. Cross-references: P11/P38/P46/P47/P48. T56–T57 are initial boundaries, not coverage of all these choices.
