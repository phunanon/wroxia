Wroxia money simulator

An agent-based Monte Carlo model of the hearth beside the haven (IV.2.2, IV.2.3.1), asking how much Nearlaw can raise from a yearly charge on every holding of hearth (demurrage), and from other charges payable only in hearth: a sales tax, a pollution charge, and, for comparison, a poll tax. A thousand adults trade with neighbours, each pricing in whichever currency costs them less, and hold hearth only for what they spend in it and for whatever savings it is cheaper to keep there. Money is in labour hours; an adult earns about 1,800 a year. Lines show the mean over the runs, with a band from the lowest to the highest run.

Assumptions

  • Adults sit on a ring, each trading with eight neighbours, a tenth of ties rewired at random. Hearth starts in one contiguous block, as in a region that takes it up first. Incomes are lognormal.
  • Each year a fifth of adults reconsider which currency they price in, choosing, with some noise, the cheaper of: exchanging what they receive in one currency but pay in the other, discounting to customers who use the other, and holding a month's receipts at the currency's holding cost (demurrage plus expected loss of value for hearth, haven inflation for havens). Buyers pay in the seller's currency.
  • Hearth balances are held only for spending in hearth: buying from those who price in it and Nearlaw's services. At no holding cost adults hold a set number of months' spending; once holding costs, they replenish more often (Baumol-Tobin), each extra exchange taking time. The land charge is paid once a year, a fifth of adults paying it, so it is bought just in time and adds little to balances.
  • Liquid savings go to whichever currency is cheaper to hold, counting the exchange saved by keeping them in the currency they will be spent in, with the semi-elasticity set below, moving a fifth of the way each year. Neither currency pays interest; if havens earn interest, read haven inflation as inflation less that interest.
  • Nearlaw spends only hearth, paying it out evenly as income. Under the fixed wage it pays a fixed hearth wage for voluntary work, so it spends what holders absorb and one hearth stays worth an hour. Under a target it spends a share of income whatever hearth is worth; holders expect hearth to lose value as fast as its stock grows, and its value is what they wish to hold divided by what is in issue. Any surplus is cancelled. Services sold for hearth are at cost, so they raise nothing net. Charges are reset each year in hearth to the share of income set.
  • Charges other than demurrage are owed yearly in hearth, set each year to the hours due, whatever currency the payer prices in, and bought just in time. Every adult sells, their turnover being their income, so a threshold bites harder than in an economy of firms. A seller owes sales tax on declared turnover once it reaches the threshold, so those just above it gain by declaring less and stay under. Sellers hide turnover where the tax saved, net of expected back tax and an equal fine, meets the cost of hiding, which rises with the square of the share hidden (hiding all of it costs half of it). Hidden turnover is found half as often for those pricing in havens, whose receipts never pass through hearth accounts; the tax, the cost of hiding, and the exchange to pay counts in each seller's choice of currency. The burden is shown where it falls by law, on sellers; if passed on to buyers, it falls nearly evenly. Polluting sellers, a set share, pay the pollution charge on their output, which shrinks with its rate by the elasticity set; abating costs nothing. The poll tax goes unpaid by anyone it would cost over a fifth of their income.
  • Left out: growth, banks and credit in either currency, interest, forgery, hoarding of notes before a stamp date, and trade abroad.
