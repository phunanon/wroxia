# One Nearlaw instead

20 November 2026, by Claude Opus, on P26, as a companion to
essays/2026-11-20-Plural-Nearlaw.md. What would follow if Wroxia kept a single
Nearlaw, and conquered, ceded, or settled land simply joined it. Nothing here is
heard or simulated, except where noted. History is from memory; check before
quoting.

## The single design

This is the draft of earlier on 20 November, before hearthlands:

- Land and people that Farlaw alone comes to hold come under Nearlaw, and then
  under Part V.
- Those who lived there, or were born there, are citizens on asking, and neither
  government may refuse them.

Everything else stays as it was before hearthlands: one Nearlaw, one body of
nearlaw, one Part V pool, one hearth. Its Hearings (T122 to T124 under that
draft) passed.

## What it does better

1. **Shorter.** It needs two clauses, not a defined term, a membership rule, a
   five-year rule, a clause that turns every "Nearlaw" plural, and edits to
   II.3.2, II.5.1, II.7.1, III.1.2, V.5.1, and Instrument 6. That is about 200
   words fewer, in a constitution that prizes being short.
2. **One class of citizen.** Every citizen has the same quota, in the same pool,
   and may take land anywhere on the day they become a citizen. There is no
   waiting period, no hearthland to belong to, and so no way to build anything
   like a homeland.
3. **Full voice at once.** Nearlaw's law-makers are drawn by lot from all
   electors (IV.3.1), so the conquered hold seats in proportion to their number
   from the first draw, with no election to win.
4. **One hearth.** The haven drift is the same problem Wroxia already has, not
   multiplied. There are no tiny hearths to fail. The first model of many
   hearths found haven's share of trade stays near 56% with one shared hearth,
   against 80% to 89% with many.
5. **No boundaries inside.** No disputes over which hearthland a matter belongs
   to, and no question of who may move where.

## What it does worse

1. **It rewards conquering empty land.** In the single pool, the share is all
   land divided by all quotas (V.1.1). Conquering a thinly peopled region makes
   every existing citizen's share bigger. Conquering a crowded one makes it
   smaller. So the single design deters conquering people but rewards conquering
   land, which is the logic of settler colonies and of _Lebensraum_. The
   author's deterrent, that the conquered vote, is weakest exactly where the
   land motive is strongest. With hearthlands, new land enlarges only its own
   people's shares, and existing citizens gain nothing from it.
2. **It lets settlers in at once.** Land falls vacant when it first comes under
   Part V, except what its occupiers take first (V.5.3). Commons, pasture used
   seasonally, absentee estates, and land whose owners fled are all vacant on
   the first day, and every existing citizen may register for them. In Algeria
   the colonists' land came largely from exactly such land: tribal commons and
   confiscated estates. With hearthlands, only the new hearthland's own citizens
   may register for its vacant land, and settlers must first live there five
   years.
3. **It imposes the conqueror's law on day one.** All of Nearlaw's existing law
   applies to the new land at once: rules of the road, building standards, the
   service hours (IV.5.2), everything. Nothing lets the conquered keep their own
   ways while they adjust. Hong Kong, Quebec, and Louisiana kept their own
   private law under a new sovereign. The author's third goal, supporting
   existing culture and governance, is served far less.
4. **The conquered are a minority in their own home.** On every local matter,
   their own roads, the use of their public land, the language of their notices,
   they are outnumbered by citizens who have never been there. Seats by lot are
   proportional, but proportional to the whole country, so local matters are
   decided mostly by outsiders. This is the grievance of every annexed region in
   a unitary state. Hearthlands give each its own law-makers.
5. **No currency valve.** A conquered region that is poorer or hit by a shock
   shares one hearth with the rest, with no exchange rate to adjust through. The
   first model found that its own hearth cut first-year unemployment after a
   shock, though less as haven took over. The cost is modest but real.
6. **It fits only conquest.** A settlement on the Moon or Mars, governed by a
   Nearlaw on Earth, would wait minutes for each word of its law and live under
   rules made for another world. A country choosing to join (essays/
   2026-11-16-Districts-and-annexation.md) would have to give up its government
   entirely, which no country has done willingly except East Germany. Joining as
   a hearthland, as a canton joins Switzerland or a state the European Union, is
   a much easier offer.

## Which matters more

| Concern                         | One Nearlaw            | Hearthlands                        |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| Length                          | shorter                | about 200 words longer             |
| Equal citizenship               | complete, at once      | full in Farlaw; land after 5 years |
| Homeland risk                   | none                   | guarded by movement and III.1.2    |
| Motive to conquer land          | rewarded               | removed                            |
| Settlers taking the land        | at once                | after five years' residence        |
| Conquered keep their own law    | no                     | yes, until changed                 |
| Local self-government           | no                     | yes                                |
| Currency                        | one hearth, less drift | many hearths, more drift, a valve  |
| Off-world and willing accession | poor fit               | good fit                           |

My judgement: the land motive decides it. A constitution whose land system makes
conquering empty land profitable for every citizen has built in the wrong
incentive, and no deterrent based on votes can offset it where there are few
people to vote. Hearthlands remove that motive, protect the conquered from
settlers, and fit willing accession and settlement too. Their costs are words,
currency, and complexity, which can be worked on. The single design's costs are
structural.

## A middle way

Some of each is possible:

- **One Nearlaw, with a closed period.** Keep the single design, but for some
  years after land comes under Part V, only those who lived there may register
  for its vacant land, and the existing law applies only as the new land's own
  electors accept. This fixes settlers and day-one law, but not the land motive
  (the share still grows), and needs its own clauses.
- **Hearthlands that may merge.** Keep hearthlands, and let two merge when the
  electors of each agree. A conquered hearthland that wants full union can
  choose it later, on equal terms, with one hearth and one pool. This keeps
  every advantage of hearthlands and lets each people decide whether to become
  single. It needs one sentence and a way for electors to agree, such as
  Instrument 6's scoring within each hearthland.

If the author wants the single design's simplicity in the long run, the second
gets there by consent rather than by conquest.
