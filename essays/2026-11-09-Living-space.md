# Living space

A model of land for Wroxia, drawn out of four rounds of quick questions on 9
November. It replaces equal shares of land as a market good with something
plainer: a slice of the world each person can close to others or open to them,
which no one can stack up.

## The aim

The wrong this model targets is dominion, not rent. In your words: "I have a
second house that this couple lives in; they haven't got a house of their own,
and so I have great dominion over them." Rent between two people who each have a
home is just association. Rent from someone with nowhere else to go is
domination.

Political philosophy calls this freedom as non-domination (Philip Pettit): you
are unfree if someone could interfere with you at will, even if they never do.
Your name for capitalism's pillar has a pedigree too: Thorstein Veblen's last
major book was _Absentee Ownership_ (1923).

The consequence is the model's central idea. **What protects a guest is the
exit, not tenant law.** A host may ask a guest to leave because the guest always
has a home of their own to go to. So the state never needs to know about the
arrangement, and Wroxia needs no law of tenancy at all.

## The model

1. **Quota.** Every adult has the same budget of points for living space. Points
   are not money and cannot be given to another person, only pooled in common.
2. **Paying.** Points are paid once, on moving in, at that day's price, which is
   set by demand. Points not spent stay free to use elsewhere. ~~If the place
   later thrives, you keep the gain; if it declines, you keep the loss.~~ On
   leaving, you get back exactly what you paid: you keep a rising area's gain
   only by living there, and moving never wins or loses you points.
3. **Title, not presence.** Your home is what your points are spent on, not
   where you happen to be. A patient in hospital, someone mid-move, or someone
   who just leaves the place empty keeps it. Leaving land empty is a right;
   empty land is only a problem where speculation exists, and here it can't.
4. **Guests.** Anyone may host anyone on any agreed terms, money included,
   informally and without the state knowing. A guest stays at the host's
   pleasure. You may also let your whole home while you are away for a time, but
   not indefinitely.
5. **Moving.** You give up all or part of what you hold and take a new place
   with the points returned. Keeping a foothold where you came from is your
   business, because it reduces what you can have anywhere else.
6. **Ties.** One mechanism lowers the points price for people with a reason to
   be somewhere. In order of strength:
   - **A named heir.** Their price is capped at their whole quota, so they can
     inherit a home worth more than a quota, but it then uses up every point
     they have.
   - **Someone named by locals,** such as the village boy away at university.
   - **A priority area.** Everyone chooses three areas and may change one every
     few years.

   Among applicants for a vacancy, the strongest tie wins, and equal ties are
   drawn by lot. Locals name individuals but cannot hand-pick among strangers. A
   nomination can be revoked until the vacancy arises, and is void if anything
   of value was given for it, so it can't become a currency and no one needs
   vetting as "really family".

7. **A home somewhere, at once.** Nearlaw keeps enough vacant homes that any
   unspent quota can become a home immediately, though not necessarily where one
   would choose. Without this, an 18-year-old with a quota but no home is
   exactly the dominated couple in the second house.
8. **Children.** Children have no quota. A household gets a smaller allowance
   for each dependant, which ends when the child comes of age with their own.
9. **Pooling.** Communes start informally, at the host's pleasure. Making one
   formal means pooling points into a co-operative, with easements and shared
   title easy to arrange. Communards can afford to leave their own land only
   because doing so doesn't leave them worse off than anyone else.

## Distortions you accept

- **Incumbents keep windfalls.** Whoever lives in a place that becomes popular
  has it better than later arrivals, and can draw income from that through
  lodgers or by letting while away. The penalty is that leaving costs them the
  advantage. You judged that enough, partly because informal letting is too
  precarious to build a living on.
- **Better, never more.** A family can keep a grand house through generations,
  but each heir spends their whole quota on it.
- **Strangers come last.** In a desirable area, someone with no ties gets
  whatever ties leave over. Three chosen priority areas mean everyone has some
  ties.
- **Guests have no security.** This is acceptable only because a home is always
  available.
- **Families with children have less space per head** than households of adults.
- **One large state machine:** the reserve of vacant homes, and someone to build
  it.

## What it would change in Wroxia

- **I.2.3** would make title rest on the quota rather than on occupancy, which
  would remain the fact of living or working somewhere. That separates the two
  jobs occupancy now does, and answers the hospital patient, the door in the
  empty building (P56), the gap mid-move (P64), and key money (P63), since
  neither land nor nominations can be sold.
- **P25** would close: points replace land value tax and its allowance, so
  nothing taxes land and P58's problem of collecting land tax disappears.
- **P62's underinvestment** eases: an orchard planted on land you hold stays
  yours while you hold it.
- **P39** would be answered for land, but not for money or things.
- The quota would be the first substantial positive privilege (II.8), and ties
  would be an instrument, not an article.

## Still open

- **Who has a quota:** citizens, residents, or every patient? Wild land and the
  animals living on it might need a share held for them by an Advocate. And if
  citizenship gives a quota, existing citizens gain from admitting fewer people.
- **Prices:** how demand sets them in practice, perhaps by periodic rounds in
  which everyone bids with equal budgets.
- **Workspace:** probably a separate, stickier quota pooled in co-operatives and
  firms, so workers can't simply walk out and no one goes "all in". Parked.
- **Leaving and dying:** whether a quota lapses on emigrating (otherwise you are
  an absentee owner abroad), and what happens to a home with no heir who takes
  it up (presumably it goes to the reserve).
- **How long "away for a time"** may last before letting a whole home becomes
  landlordism.
- **Leaving a co-operative** with your points.
- **Separation:** who keeps a pooled home when a couple or co-operative splits,
  especially where there is abuse. Formal pooling would differ from informal
  hosting.
- **Children's guardianship** generally, beyond space.

## Next

Hold the model against the Lumo essay's test: does it still leave anyone feeling
the dread of a mortgage or a landlord? Then, if you're comfortable with it, it
becomes one Problem, a few Questions, and hearing cases: a lodger asked to
leave, an heir to a farm worth more than a quota, two people with equal ties to
one cottage, and an 18-year-old claiming a home at once.
