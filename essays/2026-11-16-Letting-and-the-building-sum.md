# Letting a won plot, and the building sum

16 November 2026, by Claude Fable, on P73 and Q8 (P51). Suggestions only;
nothing here has been heard.

The author's two tests: a first-time buyer should not suffer as one does in the
United Kingdom, and whoever has worked on land should be paid for that work when
they leave.

## What a UK buyer actually pays for

A house price is two things: the building, and the right to be in that place.
The pain is nearly all the second. The place is what rises, what needs the
deposit and the thirty-year loan, and what the seller did nothing to make.

V.6.2 already cuts the two apart. The jury values what is fixed to the land "if
no one else wanted the land", so the sum is the building and never the place.
That one clause does most of what the author wants. What remains is to stop the
place being sold by another door, and to make the building easy to pay for.

## The place always tries to get sold

Wherever law holds the price of a place below what people would pay, the
difference reappears as a side payment. Rent-controlled New York and Paris had
"key money"; Japan still has it; Irish tenants sold their "tenant right" for
sums that were really the cheap rent, capitalised; Mexican ejido plots were let
though letting was banned.

In Wroxia, whoever wins a wanted plot holds a place worth more than its stake,
because the stake stops at one quota (V.3.3). The doors through which that can
be sold:

1. Letting it (P73).
2. Charging to admit someone to a pool, or to direct them (T74).
3. Asking too much for the building. V.6.2 shuts this one.
4. Being paid to decline a draw (T79).

No rule shuts every door, because a payment between two willing people can
always hide. The aim is that such payments are unenforceable, risky, and not
worth much. They are already unenforceable (I.2.5). Two things would do the
rest:

- **Risky: the occupant may take first (Q10).** If whoever has occupied land for
  a year, while no one with title has, may take it as if directed, then letting
  a whole plot hands it to the tenant. It needs no inspector and no money. It
  does not touch a lodger in the holder's own home, or a pool's admission fee,
  and probably should not: sharing the place you live in is not absentee.
- **Not worth much: a charge on contest, paid out equally (Q9, option A).** If a
  holder pays yearly what the place is worth beyond its stake, there is nothing
  left to sell. This is the land charge, valued not for revenue but as the thing
  that empties doors 1, 2, and 4 at once. It costs the Money belief something,
  since it must be paid in hearth.

I recommended dropping the charge in Q9 earlier today. Working through P73
changes that: the charge is redundant only up to the stake's ceiling, and the
plots worth letting are exactly those above it. So the choice is really this:

- The occupant rule alone: no money needed, towers and lodgers still pay for
  place.
- The charge alone, paid out equally: place is never worth selling, but wanted
  land needs hearth.
- Both.

**Suggest** the occupant rule first, heard on its own, and the charge decided
with the currency (see the essay on hearth), since a charge everyone on wanted
land owes in hearth is also a reason for hearth to be worth something.

## The building sum

Who is at risk in each design?

- **A private debt (the text now).** The leaver carries the risk that the taker
  stops paying, and the state must judge a private quarrel: was a payment
  missed, was the cow a fair instalment. It is the one enforced promise in a
  constitution that enforces no others.
- **A lump sum.** No debt, but built land goes to those with savings: the UK
  again.
- **Nearlaw in the middle.** Recommended, in this form:
  1. The leaver names a sum; the taker may challenge it before the jury, as now.
  2. When the land is taken, Nearlaw pays the leaver the whole sum at once, in
     newly issued hearth.
  3. The taker owes Nearlaw that sum as a charge on the title, in equal yearly
     parts over a term law sets, with no interest.
  4. If title ends early, for any reason, nothing more is owed. The next taker
     faces a fresh sum, of which Nearlaw keeps what it is still out and the
     leaver has the rest.

Why this shape:

- **For the buyer:** no deposit, no interest, no lender to satisfy, and one can
  walk away owing nothing. It is renting to own, with no landlord.
- **For the leaver:** paid in full on the day, whatever the taker later does.
  Work on land is remunerated, as the author wants.
- **For Nearlaw:** it pays only when someone has actually taken the land at that
  sum, so it never holds unsold buildings. Its risk is a building losing value
  between one taker and the next.
- **For the text:** the debt becomes a charge, and charges already have their
  machinery: unpaid, they end title as on notice (V.8.3). "Was a payment missed"
  is answered from Nearlaw's own books. The private debt, and Q8's four loose
  ends, go.
- **For hearth:** every built plot creates hearth when it changes hands, and a
  holder who must find hearth each year to retire it. That is how bank money
  works everywhere, without the interest. And lending without interest is no
  sacrifice in a currency that already loses value each year (IV.2.3.1).

What it costs:

- Built land then needs hearth, which strains the Money belief. Unbuilt land and
  starter plots (V.3.4) still need none, and nothing stops leaver and taker
  settling privately, in kind, with the sum set at nothing; such a deal is
  simply not enforced.
- The jury becomes the guard of the public purse, not only of the taker. Five
  electors may be too few when the taker has no reason to object, since Nearlaw
  pays first. Nearlaw should be able to challenge a sum as the taker can.
- A sum paid "as they directed" at death passes money on, which is P39.

Old English farm law is the nearest precedent: from 1875 a departing tenant was
owed the value of their improvements "to an incoming tenant", set by local
valuers, and it worked for a century. The rule that an eccentric improvement is
worth only what the next person would pay for it comes from there, and V.6.2 has
it already.
