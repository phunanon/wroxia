# Plural Nearlaw

20 November 2026, by Claude Opus, on P26. What it takes for conquered, ceded, or
settled land to have a Nearlaw of its own under the one Farlaw, what that
touches in the text, and the choices it leaves the author. History is from
memory; check before quoting. Nothing here is heard.

## The problem

The author wants three things at once:

1. **No underclass.** Conquered people must not end up ruled without a voice, as
   Algeria's Muslims were under the _indigénat_ (French nationals, but not
   citizens, under a separate penal code, until 1946).
2. **Conquest should cost the conqueror.** If the conquered vote in Farlaw,
   Farlaw thinks twice before conquering.
3. **Existing land is protected.** A conquest should not dilute existing
   citizens' land shares, or hand the conquered a claim on land far from home.

A single Nearlaw can give the first two only by giving up the third: the
conquered become citizens, take quotas, and the share shrinks everywhere. A
plural Nearlaw gives all three: each body of land and people governs its own
land and shares, and everyone shares one Farlaw.

## The danger in it

Separate land pools for conquered people, with the conqueror's land kept apart,
is the shape of South Africa's homelands and of reservations: people confined to
poor land while the good land stays with others. What made the homelands an
injustice was that their people were stripped of national citizenship and of
free movement. So the plural design is safe only if:

- the conquered are full citizens of the whole (Farlaw votes, Advocates,
  amendments, II.5), which IV.2.7 already gives;
- anyone may move, live, and work in any part, and trade across without duties;
- no law treats people differently by which part they belong to, except for land
  and what goes with it;
- membership of a part can change, so no one is stuck in one forever.

## Precedents

- **Swiss cantons** and **US states** share a federal government and free
  movement. Membership follows residence, and land is not pooled, so nothing
  like Part V's share arises.
- **The EU** keeps national citizenship beside a shared one, with free movement
  and residence, and lets members limit buying land for a time after joining
  (Denmark keeps rules on second homes to this day).
- **Åland** is part of Finland, but only those with a "right of domicile",
  gained after five years' residence, may own land there. This is the closest
  precedent to the protection the author wants, with a waiting period rather
  than a veto.
- **Hong Kong, Quebec, and Louisiana** kept their own private law under a new
  sovereign, which bears on what a new part starts with.
- **US territories**: Puerto Rico's people are US citizens with no vote for
  Congress or the president. That is the opposite of what Wroxia wants, and a
  warning against any half-membership.

## A design

**Terms.** Keep _Farlaw_ and _Nearlaw_ as the two kinds of government, and add
one word for a body of land and people with its own Nearlaw, defined in Part I.
Candidates: _shire_ (plain, old, self-governing, with no colonial ring), _hold_,
or _hearthland_ (matching the hearth). The author chose _hearthland_, which fits
each one issuing its own hearth. Everyone is a citizen of Wroxia, and each
citizen belongs to exactly one hearthland.

**One clause carries most of it.** Rather than edit every mention of Nearlaw, a
single clause says: where this Constitution speaks of Nearlaw, it means each
hearthland's Nearlaw, for that hearthland, its land, and its citizens. Part V's
share, quotas, starter plots, licence rent, service, the hearth, and law-making
then all work per hearthland with no other change. This is the convention of
putting what several articles share in one place.

**How hearthlands arise.** Land that comes under Wroxia, whether by force, by
treaty, or by settlement (a seastead, the Moon), forms a new hearthland, unless
an existing hearthland's Nearlaw and the new land's people both agree that it
join that hearthland instead. Its citizens are those who lived there when it
came under Wroxia, or were born there. A citizen of another hearthland among
them chooses which to belong to.

**What it starts with.** A new hearthland keeps the law it had, so far as this
Constitution allows (III.1.5 already voids the rest), until its own law-making
body changes it. Empty or newly settled land starts with no law but this
Constitution. Farlaw keeps order there until the hearthland's Nearlaw first
sits, which must be within a set time.

**Moving.** A citizen may live and work in any hearthland. They join another
hearthland, leaving their own, after living there five years (Åland's figure)
and asking. The author ruled out joining sooner with a Nearlaw's consent: "great
injustices can occur for those who had to wait and those who didn't." A child
belongs to a parent's hearthland.

**Between hearthlands.** Crossing between hearthlands is not crossing Wroxia's
border, so Farlaw levies no duty on it. Each Nearlaw governs nearlaw matters on
its own land. The boundary court decides which government a matter belongs to,
whether the dispute is between Farlaw and a hearthland or between two
hearthlands.

**Joining and leaving** (not drafted). Two hearthlands might merge when the
electors of each agree. This gives a route to full union, which Algeria was
promised (as departments) but never had on equal terms. Secession is a separate
question, and this design leaves it open.

## Many hearths, one haven

The author asked for this to be thought through, since it has never been
modelled. Nothing below is simulated.

**What the drafted text does.** IV.2.2 with IV.2.8 gives each hearthland's
Nearlaw its own hearth, issued through its own office. IV.2.3 makes each
hearthland's charges payable only in its own hearth, and IV.5.1.3's demurrage
runs at one rate for each hearth. Farlaw's haven is one currency for all of
Wroxia. There is still no legal tender, so anyone may price and save in any
hearth, in haven, or in foreign money. Wroxia becomes several floating hearths
beside one haven.

**What gives each hearth value.** Each hearth is worth what people owe in it:
charges for land its Nearlaw keeps, the fixed sum from licence rent, demurrage,
charges on risky acts, and the building charge if that is adopted. All of these
arise from land and acts in that hearthland, so each hearth is roughly worth
what living there requires. A small, poor, newly conquered hearthland's hearth
has a small but real base.

### What separate hearths do well

1. **They absorb shocks.** Mundell's theory of optimal currency areas says
   regions that suffer different shocks, and whose people cannot easily move
   between them, do better with their own exchange rate. The five-year rule
   deliberately slows moving land between hearthlands, so hearthlands are poor
   candidates for one shared money. When a hearthland's trade collapses, its
   hearth falls and its prices adjust, rather than its jobs. Southern Italy
   inside the lira, and Greece inside the euro, had no such valve.
2. **No conversion on the first day.** East Germany's 1990 switch to the
   Deutschmark at a generous political rate priced its firms out of business
   within months. Here there is no switch: the conquered keep whatever money
   they used, since nothing is legal tender, and their own hearth starts when
   their Nearlaw first spends it. The market sets its value from the start.
3. **No common pool to raid.** After 1991, fifteen post-Soviet central banks
   could all create roubles, and each gained by issuing more, since the
   inflation fell on all of them. That ended in hyperinflation and the end of
   the rouble zone in 1993. Separate hearths avoid this: a Nearlaw that issues
   too much sinks its own hearth, in public, and harms only those who chose to
   hold it. That is the hearth-and-haven discipline (essay of 16 November),
   repeated across hearthlands.

### What they risk

1. **Haven becomes everyone's money.** Traders across hearthlands want one unit,
   and haven is the only one good everywhere. It also carries no demurrage.
   Expect inter-hearthland trade and savings to drift into haven, as Cambodia's
   did into dollars. That makes Farlaw, the government meant to be small in
   daily life, the issuer of the money people save and trade in. Its seigniorage
   grows, which weakens the 16 November point that Farlaw cannot finance war
   with the money houses are priced in. The guards would be a demurrage on haven
   too (IV.5.3 now forbids Farlaw any charge but border duties), or a cap on
   haven issued. I suggest modelling this before adding either guard.
2. **Small hearths are costly to use.** A seastead of fifty people cannot
   sustain a market in its own money. Panama, Ecuador, and Kiribati use another
   country's currency instead. The text could let a Nearlaw take another
   hearthland's hearth as its own, with that Nearlaw's agreement, so that its
   charges are payable in it. The adopter then gains nothing from issue, and its
   money's supply is set by another hearthland, which is the price of
   convenience.
3. **Pegging.** A Nearlaw could spend its hearth buying another's to hold its
   rate. Cuba's and China's fixed internal rates bred black markets and were
   abandoned. With no legal tender a peg can never be forced on private trade,
   so no text is needed. A peg that fails costs only the Nearlaw that tried it.
4. **Merging.** If hearthlands may merge, their hearths must become one. A
   floating rate on the day of the merger gives a market conversion rate, which
   East Germany lacked.

### Pinning a hearth to an hour helps more now

The 16 November essay proposed that Nearlaw pay one hearth for one hour of work.
If every Nearlaw did, and anyone may do such work in any hearthland (III.1.2),
then no hearth could cost more than an hour anywhere. Rates between hearths
would stay near par without a peg, and would move only by how much each
hearthland charges and holds. That makes the hour a common anchor for many
hearths. It is not in the text.

### What to model

The money simulator models one hearth beside the haven. To test the drafted
text, it needs:

- Two or more hearthlands of different sizes and productivity, with one haven.
- Exchange rates and their volatility.
- The share of trade and savings that ends up in haven.
- Demurrage raised in each hearthland.
- A shock to one hearthland, compared against the same shock under one shared
  hearth issued per citizen.
- Whether a hearthland of a hundred people can keep its hearth in use.

### First model (20 November)

A first run of moneysim's new multi-hearthland model (workflows/runs/
moneysim-hearthlands.md: hearthlands of 1,000, 300, and 100 adults, 3%
demurrage, Nearlaw paying a fixed hearth wage, 20 runs) roughly confirms both
sides:

- **Haven drift is real.** About 64% of savings sit in haven from the first
  decade. Trade drifts there over 50 years, to 80% within hearthlands and 89%
  between them. With one shared hearth it stays near 56%. Every point of
  demurrage adds about 8 points of savings in haven.
- **The valve works, but only for those still pricing in hearth.** After a 30%
  shock to one hearthland, its own hearth cut first-year extra unemployment from
  about 25% to 20% (fixed wage) or 14% to 8% (floating), and by more when most
  priced in hearth. It weakens as haven takes over.
- **Tiny hearths die.** A 100-person hearthland's own hearth falls from 42% use
  to 3% by year 50. Adopting a neighbour's keeps it at 23%.
- **A fixed hearth wage pins every hearth at an hour,** so rates between hearths
  stay at par, as the hour-anchor section expects.

The model is crude in ways that matter. Whether a hearth survives turns on how
many price in it at the start, which the model takes as given. Its unemployment
rests on an assumed wage stickiness. Its exchange rates ignore trade balances.
It leaves out migration, Farlaw's haven issue, and other charges. Its numbers
are leads, not findings. Together they suggest the haven-dominance guard
deserves a decision: demurrage on haven, or a charge such as the building charge
large enough to keep hearth in use.

## Left open

- **Proof of having lived there**, for people without papers. Courts decide it
  as a fact, but a conquering Farlaw's officials would be the first to ask.
- **Amendments.** All of Wroxia's electors amend everything, so a large
  hearthland could rewrite Part V for a small one. One guard is that an
  amendment touching only some hearthlands needs their electors too.
- **Small hearthlands.** Instrument 1's minimum seats keep a town's body
  workable, but a hearthland of a dozen settlers is barely a government.
- **Joint conquest.** IV.2.7 covers only land Farlaw holds alone. Land held with
  an ally is not covered.
- **Secession.** May a hearthland leave Wroxia, and if so, by what vote?
