# Districts, communities, joining, and starting small

16 November 2026, by Claude Fable. A first map of ground the author has not yet
covered: local government, the place of existing peoples and institutions, how a
country might join Wroxia, and how Wroxia might begin as a few dozen people. It
offers choices, not answers. History is from memory; check before quoting. I
have not read P26 (territory and conquest), which bears on this.

## Local government matters less in Wroxia than anywhere else

Roadmap.md marks local government _out_. That is more defensible than it looks,
for two reasons peculiar to this text.

- **No government may forbid a harmless act** (III.1.1). The misery of local
  rule elsewhere, the residents' association that bans washing lines, cannot
  happen at any level. What is left for a local body to decide is how land kept
  for public use is used, what local work service hours do, and what is spent.
- **Anyone can leave, at once.** Every district keeps starter plots (V.3.4). In
  most countries moving away from bad local rule costs a house; here it costs a
  season. Local variety is therefore much safer than usual.

So the stakes are low, and the choice can be made lightly.

## Four shapes

1. **None.** One Nearlaw; districts are lines on the land office's map (V.1.4).
   Shortest. But a body by lot from the whole country decides one village's
   footpaths, and service hours are directed from far away, which history says
   people resent (essays/2026-11-16-Precedents.md).
2. **Places.** Each district has its own small body by lot, deciding the use of
   public land there and the local use of service hours, within Nearlaw's law.
   Swiss communes work like this. It is one sentence citing Instrument 1.
3. **Commons.** Those who use a street, a park, or a water source govern it, as
   in Elinor Ostrom's studies (P3). Closest to the beliefs, hardest to write:
   who is a user, and how do they decide?
4. **Chosen communities** (P31), not tied to place. They need almost no text,
   because pools (V.4) already let people hold land together and make rules by
   consent. What they cannot do is bind anyone who has withdrawn consent
   (I.2.5), so they are safe by construction.

These combine. **A suggestion:** shape 2 for public land and hours, shape 4 for
everything else, and nothing in the constitution about shape 4 beyond what pools
already give.

## Existing peoples and institutions

The author's third goal is that Wroxia support culture, difference, language,
and existing governance. Ask of any existing body what it keeps and loses.

It keeps: its land, if its members pool their quotas there; its rules, for those
who consent; its language, faith, schools, and customs, none of which law may
forbid.

It loses: any power over someone who says no. No tithe or tax, no punishment, no
holding of members, children included once they can act for themselves (IV.5;
I.2.6 until 17 November).

So a parish council, a monastery, a kibbutz, a First Nation, or a canton can
continue as a community, and cannot continue as a government. For most, that is
survivable and even welcome. The flashpoints will be:

- **Animals.** No one may hold an animal as a thing (II.1.5). Herding peoples,
  ritual slaughter, and every farm are affected on the first day (P78). This is
  the largest cultural cost in the text, and for pastoral peoples it is the
  whole of their way of life.
- **Children.** Nothing may be done to a child except for their own sake
  (II.2.8). Circumcision, arranged betrothal, and withholding schooling or
  medicine will all be argued.
- **Land larger than quotas.** A people holding wide territory in common holds
  more than its members' shares. Whether ancestral land needs its own rule is a
  question for the author.
- **Authority.** Chiefs, elders, and clergy keep respect, not command.

## Joining

History's cases of one legal order replacing another overnight:

- **East Germany, 1990.** Took on West German law in a day. Its money was
  converted at a flattering rate, which priced its factories out of business
  within months. Wroxia has an advantage here: with no legal tender, old money
  simply goes on being used beside the new, at whatever rate people give it.
- **Land to the tiller: Japan (1946), Taiwan (1953), South Korea.** Tenants were
  given the land they farmed, quickly, under clear rules, and landlords were
  paid something (in bonds, or shares in state firms). All three are counted
  among the most successful reforms of the century. **Zimbabwe (2000)** did it
  without rules or payment, and farm output collapsed.
- **The European Union.** New members adopt the whole body of law, but over
  years, with agreed delays for the hard parts.
- **Hong Kong, Louisiana, Quebec.** Kept their old private law for decades or
  for good under a new sovereign.

What Wroxia's text does on the day (V.5.3, P80): whoever occupies land may take
it first. Occupiers are those who live or work there (I.2.3), so tenants and
farm workers take, and landlords and lenders do not. Mortgages, leases, and
debts end with nothing owed. Animals are free. Most prisoners must be released,
since confinement is allowed only in defence (II.2.3).

The winners are renters, the indebted, and the landless. The losers are
landlords, banks, and above all anyone whose pension is a claim on a fund that
holds loans and rented buildings. That last group is large, old, and blameless,
and will decide whether a country ever votes to join. **The questions for the
author:**

1. Is anything paid to those who lose? The successful land reforms paid
   something. Nearlaw could pay in new hearth, as with building sums.
2. Does it happen in a day, or by stages, with dates set in the act of joining?
3. May a joining country keep some of its old law for a time, as Hong Kong did?
   The text says a law contrary to the constitution has no force (III.1.5), so
   as written, no.
4. Is joining by a vote of the people there, and by what majority?

## Starting small

Nobody has founded a recognised state on empty land in living memory. Sealand
and Liberland are claims no one honours. What has worked is a community inside a
host country:

- **Twin Oaks** (Virginia, since 1967): about a hundred people, land and income
  in common, and a labour quota of about forty hours a week counted in credits.
  It is the longest-running hours economy there is.
- **Marinaleda** (Spain): the town gives land and materials, families build
  their own houses with their neighbours' labour, and then pay a few euros a
  month. This is the building charge, working, for forty years.
- **Christiania** (Copenhagen, since 1971): common land, decisions by consensus,
  open sale of cannabis. It lasted, but the drug trade drew gangs it could not
  expel, and in 2012 it made peace by buying its land.
- **The kibbutzim**: flourished for two generations, then mostly privatised when
  the young left.

The lesson fits Wroxia's own design. Nearly everything in Nearlaw can exist
without sovereignty: pooled land, quotas, draws, hearth as a local currency in
hours, a council by lot, service hours, restitution among members. Nearly
everything in Farlaw cannot: borders, forces, treaties. So the two governments
are also two stages. **A founding group can be a Nearlaw first**, as an
association owning land under a host country's law, with the constitution as its
charter, and gain a Farlaw only if it ever gains a border.

Under a host, some of the text must wait: drugs stay illegal, the host's courts
enforce debts, its child law overrides IV.5 (I.2.6 until 17 November), and it
taxes in its own money. Which articles are suspended, and how members agree to
that, would be the charter's one addition. Whether the constitution should say
anything about its own founding is open; most do not.
