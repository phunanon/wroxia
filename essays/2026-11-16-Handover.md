the author. This says what changed, what must be heard, what I did not read,
where I was unsure, and what I would do next. Trust the Constitution, Problems,
and Questions over this essay wherever they differ; it will age.

## About the author

Recorded in memory too. Three goals: the text matches their beliefs and can be
advocated whole or in part; people and small models can digest it; most people,
seeing the whole, would not turn up their noses. Beliefs.md was written after
most of the text and still grows. They have no legal training and no children,
and say so. Open questions tire them; a named person in a concrete fix, with two
or three ways out, does not. They often answer a choice with a better question;
when that happens, stop asking and talk.

"Absentee ownership" is their name for what they oppose in capitalism. A sharper
wording I offered, which they have not adopted: the law should never enforce an
income from owning what others use. The text nearly achieves that already: land
at once for everyone, quotas that cannot be transferred, no promise enforced,
money that decays.

## Choices that look like accidents and are not

From the Problems I read. Do not simplify these away without asking.

- **Service cannot be bought off or done by another** (V.8.1). This is what
  separates it from every hated labour duty in history.
- **Starter plots carry no service.** That free way out is what keeps service
  from being compelled work (II.1.3), which no amendment can touch.
- **The jury values a building "if no one else wanted the land"** (V.6.2). It is
  the clause that stops location being sold.
- **Consent stands until withdrawn, whatever was promised** (I.2.5). The whole
  absence of contract law rests on it.
- **Each government taxes only in its own currency** (IV.2.3), and what public
  land earns beyond its cost is paid out equally, so Nearlaw never profits from
  congestion or vacancy.
- **Farlaw is small on purpose** (P50).

## Where I was unsure, or wrong

- **The land charge.** I recommended sharing it out, then dropping it, then
  keeping it, then dropping it. The last rests on the author's argument about
  letting. What nothing now answers: a wanted plot handed down a family for one
  quota a generation (P39), and contest above the stake's ceiling of one quota.
- **Every figure and date in today's essays is from memory.** I searched
  nothing. The sizes I gave for Nearlaw's income (4% to 5%), for unrecovered
  restitution (0.5% to 2%), and for the hearth charge on a housing-sized stock
  (1% to 2%) are estimates, not simulations.
- **"A hearth is an hour" and the building charge** are the two ideas I think
  matter most, and neither has met a simulator. The hearth essay says what to
  run.
- **Starter houses break the building charge** as I described it (see the worked
  essay, first unsolved point). I found this late.
