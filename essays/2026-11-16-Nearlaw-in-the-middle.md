# Nearlaw in the middle, worked through

16 November 2026, by Claude Fable, for whoever next discusses Q8 with the
author. The author liked this design but said they did not fully understand it.
This essay explains it slowly, with one example carried all the way through, and
lists what is unsolved. It is a proposal: nothing is drafted, simulated, or
heard. Hearth is counted in hours here, as Q13 proposes; the design works the
same if it is not.

## The problem it solves

When someone leaves land, they may ask a sum for what they built on it (V.6.1).
The next person to take the land is drawn by lot, so they may have no savings.
Today the text lets the taker pay the leaver "yearly if they wish", and ends the
taker's title if a payment is missed. That makes the leaver a lender who waits
years for their money, and makes the state referee of a private debt in a
constitution that enforces no other promise (P51).

The proposal: Nearlaw stands between them. The leaver is paid by Nearlaw. The
taker pays Nearlaw.

## One house, three people

**Mari** builds a house on her plot and later gives notice. She asks 4,000
hearth for the house. The sum is shown to everyone who registers for the plot
(V.6.3).

**Tomos** is drawn. He may accept the 4,000 or offer less, in which case the
jury of five decides as now (V.6.2). Say 4,000 stands, and Tomos takes the plot.

_On that day:_

- Nearlaw creates 4,000 new hearth and gives them to Mari. She is paid in full
  and has nothing more to do with the plot.
- Tomos's title now carries a **building charge** of 4,000, payable to Nearlaw
  in equal yearly parts over a term law sets. Say twenty years: 200 a year. No
  interest.

_Each year:_ Tomos pays 200 hearth. Nearlaw does not spend them. It cancels
them, because they repay hearth it created. After six years he has paid 1,200,
and 2,800 is still outstanding on the plot.

_Tomos leaves._ He has added a workshop. He gives notice and asks 4,500.

**Sara** is drawn, accepts 4,500, and takes the plot. On that day:

- Nearlaw creates 4,500 hearth. It keeps and cancels 2,800, which clears what
  was outstanding. Tomos gets the other 1,700.
- Sara's title carries a building charge of 4,500: 225 a year.

Where Tomos ends up: he paid in 1,200 and got out 1,700. The difference of 500
is his workshop. His six years in the house cost him nothing, because the house
held its value. If it had worn and the jury had put it at 3,700, he would have
got 900, and his six years would have cost him 300. **So the cost of living in a
house is its wear, and nothing else:** no price for the place, no interest, no
deposit.

## The same thing as a rule

1. Whoever leaves names a sum. The taker may challenge it before the jury.
2. When the land is taken, Nearlaw pays the leaver the sum, less whatever is
   still outstanding on the plot.
3. The taker owes Nearlaw the sum as a charge on the title, in yearly parts.
4. If the taker stops paying, their title ends, as on notice. They owe nothing
   more. Step 1 then happens with them as the leaver.

The debt belongs to the plot, not the person. Nobody can carry debt from plot to
plot, because leaving always settles it out of the next sum.

## What it is like, and unlike

- **Like a mortgage,** in that a house is paid for over years out of income.
- **Unlike a mortgage:** there is no interest; no deposit; no bank deciding who
  qualifies; no debt that follows you if you leave; and no price for the
  location, since the jury values the building "if no one else wanted the land".
- **Like how banks make money.** A bank that grants a mortgage creates the money
  it lends, and the money vanishes as the loan is repaid. Nearlaw would do the
  same, openly. Its books always balance: hearth in people's hands on one side,
  building charges people owe on the other.

This is also why the design matters for the currency (P35). Hearth has value
only if people need it for something. Everyone living in a house someone else
built would need hearth every year. See essays/2026-11-16-Hearth-and-haven.md.

## Questions the author asked, and the answers given

- **"What stops people building big and leaving?"** Nearlaw pays only when
  someone has taken the plot at that sum. A mansion nobody will take at its
  price earns its builder nothing until they lower the sum. The builder carries
  that risk, as now.
- **"Does the state say what it is willing to pay?"** No. The taker and the jury
  set the sum, as now. The taker still cares, because they repay it.
- **"What stops people doing it privately?"** Nothing. Mari may accept Tomos's
  promise, or his cow, and set the sum at nothing. But no promise binds (P51),
  so if he stops, she has no remedy. Nearlaw is the only party with a remedy
  that needs no contract law: a charge on the title.
- **"What about payment in kind?"** Privately, at the leaver's risk; or, if a
  hearth is an hour, by working for Nearlaw for hearth.

## What is not solved

Each of these should become a case or a simulation before anything is drafted.

1. **Starter houses.** A landless person takes a starter plot and is asked
   nothing for the house on it (V.3.4), yet may later ask a sum for it (T93,
   P85). Today the next taker pays that sum. If Nearlaw paid it at once, taking
   a free house, leaving, and collecting would pay well. Either starter houses
   carry no sum until improved, or P85's decision needs revisiting.
2. **A house worth less than what is outstanding.** If it burns, or is stripped,
   the next sum does not cover what Nearlaw is owed. Nearlaw then has hearth in
   circulation that no one will repay, which everyone bears through prices.
   Someone who wrecks a house they still owe on has harmed no one the text
   recognises.
3. **The jury guards public money.** When the taker repays over twenty years and
   can walk away, they have less reason to fight a high sum than a buyer paying
   cash. Nearlaw should be able to challenge a sum too. Collusion between leaver
   and taker (a friend "takes" at a high sum, the leaver is paid, the friend
   walks away) needs a case: it fails if the plot is truly drawn by lot, and may
   succeed where the taker is directed (V.4.2).
4. **Directions and estates.** A sum may be paid "as they directed", so at death
   Nearlaw would pay an heir the value of the house, while a directed taker
   (perhaps the same heir) owes it back over twenty years. That is a loan to
   every heir. It may be fine; it is P39's business.
5. **Who pays for building in the first place.** The design pays for a house
   once it changes hands. It lends nothing to build one. Builders save, pool, or
   build slowly with their own hands.
6. **The term and the cap.** Law sets the years. Whether the constitution should
   fix a longest term, or forbid interest in words, is open.
7. **The Money belief.** Built land would need hearth. Unbuilt land and starter
   plots would not. The author should say whether that is acceptable.
8. **Who is "Nearlaw" here.** The body that creates hearth must not be the body
   that spends it (IV.2.2 as drafted on 16 November). The building charge is the
   issuer's business, not the treasury's.

## What to ask the author next

- Is a house you can walk away from, owing nothing, what you want? It is
  generous to takers and puts the risk of a falling house on everyone.
- Should the first taker of a starter house be able to ask a sum for it?
- Is twenty years of 200 hours a year what you picture for a modest house? It is
  about a tenth of a working year, where UK households pay a third.
