# One government for X, another for Y

16 November 2026, by Claude Fable, on P30, P31, P36, and P50. Whether dividing
government by subject works, what the world's attempts show, and where Nearlaw
could be divided further. History and figures are from memory; check before
quoting.

## The idea has a name, and a literature

- **Polycentric government.** Vincent and Elinor Ostrom argued from 1961 that
  many overlapping bodies, each sized to its task, can serve people better than
  one tidy hierarchy. Elinor Ostrom's studies of American police found small
  overlapping forces did at least as well as merged ones.
- **Functional, overlapping, competing jurisdictions.** The economists Bruno
  Frey and Reiner Eichenberger proposed in the 1990s that each public task have
  its own government, with its own members, votes, and taxes.
- **Two types of government.** Liesbet Hooghe and Gary Marks (2003) sorted all
  arrangements into general-purpose governments nested by territory (nation,
  region, town) and task-specific ones that cut across. Most countries are the
  first kind with patches of the second.
- **Demarchy.** The Australian philosopher John Burnheim (_Is Democracy
  Possible?_, 1985) proposed abolishing the general-purpose state for many
  bodies, one per function, each chosen by lot from those it affects. This is
  the closest published design to Wroxia, and worth the author's reading before
  any other book.

So the vague feeling is a respectable position. The case for it:

- An election bundles everything. A voter who likes one party's schools and
  another's roads cannot say so. Separate bodies unbundle the choice.
- No one body commands everything, which is the Separated domains and Private
  domination beliefs.
- A body with one task can be judged on it.

## What happened when it was tried

- **Victorian Britain** governed by function: elected school boards, poor-law
  guardians, highway boards, sanitary boards, burial boards, each with its own
  area, election, and tax. A minister of the time called it "a chaos of areas, a
  chaos of authorities, and a chaos of rates". Between 1888 and 1902 nearly all
  were merged into general councils.
- **The United States** has about 39,000 special districts (water, fire,
  libraries, mosquitoes) and 12,000 school districts, most elected. Turnout is
  often under a tenth. The few who vote are those with a stake: teachers in
  school elections, developers in water districts. Districts that tax the same
  people each take a little more than one government would (Christopher Berry,
  _Imperfect Union_, 2009).
- **England's police commissioners** (2012), each elected for policing alone,
  drew a 15% turnout at their first election.
- **Belgium** has governments by territory (Regions, for the economy) and by
  language (Communities, for schools and culture), so a person in Brussels lives
  under several at once by subject. It functions, and protects minorities well,
  but it is slow and few Belgians can explain it.
- **Switzerland** has school communes and church communes with their own budgets
  and votes beside the ordinary commune. They work, in a country where people
  vote four times a year by habit.
- **Central banks, courts, and auditors** are single-task bodies almost
  everywhere, and they are the clear successes: independent central banks keep
  prices steadier than finance ministries did.

Four lessons:

1. **Attention runs out.** People will not follow many elections. Bodies nobody
   watches are captured by whoever cares most.
2. **Nobody weighs one task against another.** A schools body wants more for
   schools. Either something above them divides the money, or each taxes for
   itself and together they take too much.
3. **Borders between subjects are fought over.** P50 records how the American
   federal government grew through one elastic clause.
4. **It works where the task is narrow, the measure of success is plain, and the
   body is not elected:** money, judging, auditing.

## Wroxia is unusually well placed

Three of the four are answered by what the constitution already has.

- **Attention:** bodies drawn by lot (Instrument 1) need no turnout. This was
  Burnheim's whole point. One caution: reconfirmation asks every elector to
  score members of every body each year (Instrument 1.3). With many bodies, that
  becomes the Victorian ballot again. Scoring might be left to a sample drawn by
  lot.
- **Borders:** a matter belongs to a government "by what it concerns", and a
  boundary court decides (IV.2.5). That already scales to more than two.
- **Narrow tasks:** the four Advocates are already functional bodies, each "an
  office, headed by a body chosen by Instrument 1" (IV.4.4).

The unanswered one is the second: who divides the means. Wroxia's means are
mostly hours (V.9), which suggests an answer no country could use: let each
person say which office their hours go to, and let the office choose the work.
Offices that people think matter get the labour. It needs thought (the
unglamorous offices may starve) but it would give "vote for X separately" a
direct meaning, with no election at all.

## Split the doing, not the law-making

There are two things one might split, and they behave differently.

- **Law-making by subject** is what Nearlaw and Farlaw do. Each further split
  adds a border to fight over, and another body of law for people to know,
  against the Preamble. Farlaw earns its border because what lies behind it is
  dangerous: armies, treaties, the frontier. Its smallness is not a
  disappointment. The early American federal government was meant to be this
  small and failed to stay so; a Farlaw that has stayed small is the design
  working. I would not split law-making again.
- **Doing by task** is cheap and has good precedent. Nearlaw currently has a
  law-making body and nothing else: no executive at all (P30). Rather than one
  Steward, its work could be done by offices on the Advocates' pattern, each
  bound by Nearlaw's law but not run by its law-makers.

Candidates, in order of how strong the case is:

1. **The hearth office.** Issues hearth, pays the hourly wage, takes the hearth
   charge. Separating the issuer from the spender is the best-proven split there
   is. Without it, Nearlaw's law-makers can print what they cannot raise.
2. **The land office.** Runs draws, registers, juries, and building charges.
   Nearlaw "takes land like anyone" (V.3.1) in draws that Nearlaw runs; whoever
   holds the lottery should not hold a ticket.
3. **The restitution office.** Pays those harmed, disputes doubtful claims,
   recovers, and keeps the fund (see essays/2026-11-16-Hearth-and-haven.md).
4. **Courts** (P46), including the boundary court (P10).
5. **The service office.** Arranges hours. It has "the incentive to pick the
   best roles per person" only if it is judged on that and nothing else.
6. **Force.** The hardest. Every tradition puts police under whoever answers for
   the law. Leave it with P30.

By the project's own convention this is one instrument, not six articles: an
instrument defining an office (headed by a body under Instrument 1, bound by
law, paid as law provides, unable to do another office's work), which IV.4.4 and
each new office would cite.

Schools, clinics, and other services are a different matter. They are better
done near where people live, or by the communities people choose (P31), and that
is the question of districts and communes, not of functions.

## For the author

- Read Burnheim. Then decide whether Wroxia is "two governments" or "two
  law-makers and many offices". I think it is already the second, and saying so
  would settle P30.
- The benefit claimed for functional splits that best survives the evidence is
  not better services. It is that no one can capture everything at once. That is
  a reason the author already holds, and it is enough.
