# Hearth and haven

16 November 2026, by Claude Fable, on P35, P82, P12, and Q8. On what gives
hearth value, which charges can use it without distortion, whether Nearlaw can
afford to be an insurer, and what two currencies can do for each other. Nothing
here is simulated or heard; the economics and history are from memory. Each
section ends with what to test.

## The author is right about value

Money a state merely prints and hands out is worth what a new cryptocurrency is
worth. What gives it value is that people must get hold of it to settle
something they owe the issuer. P35 reached this already, and the simulators
confirmed it: "taxes owed in hearth fix its value".

Wroxia adds a twist. No one may be compelled to work or to join a scheme of
exchange (II.1.3), so there is no legal tender, and no tax that everyone must
pay. Two consequences:

- **Gresham's law runs backwards.** "Bad money drives out good" holds only where
  law forces sellers to take the bad money. Where they may refuse, good money
  drives out bad: sellers ask for haven, and hearth dies. The simulator's
  cascade above 10% demurrage is this.
- **Every hearth debt must be the price of something a person chose.** The
  question is not "which tax?" but "what does Nearlaw offer that people want
  enough to owe hearth for?"

## What people could owe in hearth

Sorted by how much demand each makes, with whether it bends anyone's choices.

1. **The building charge** (the Nearlaw-in-the-middle design in
   essays/2026-11-16-Letting-and-the-building-sum.md). By far the largest.
   Housing is the biggest thing any household pays for; in the United Kingdom
   outstanding mortgages are about two thirds of a year's national income. If
   Nearlaw pays leavers in new hearth and takers repay it yearly, then a large
   stock of hearth exists, and every hearth in it is matched by a hearth someone
   owes. It raises nothing itself, since what is repaid cancels what was issued.
   But it makes hearth necessary to anyone living in a house they did not build,
   and it distorts nothing: it is the price of the house.
2. **The hearth charge** (demurrage, IV.2.3.1). This is the revenue. The
   simulator found 0.3% to 0.6% of income, because little hearth was held. On a
   stock the size of the housing debt, 3% a year would be nearer 1% to 2% of
   income. It bends choices a little, towards spending and away from hoarding,
   which the author wants.
3. **Risk levies.** III.1.1 lets law forbid kinds of act that put others at
   unreasonable risk. Law can instead allow them to whoever pays a levy in
   hearth towards the harm such acts cause: driving (P42), keeping dangerous
   machines, storing fuel. Economists call this a corrective charge: it makes
   choices better, not worse. It is paid by choice, and it funds restitution
   (below).
4. **The land charge on contest, paid out equally** (Q9, option A). Small, but
   the textbook undistorting charge, and it puts hearth in every pocket each
   year.
5. **Charges for land Nearlaw keeps** (tolls, moorings, stalls): already in,
   already paid out beyond cost.
6. **Keeping promises.** Nothing in Wroxia makes a promise bind (P51), and five
   of eight residents were hurt by that. Nearlaw could offer to hold things in
   trust for a fee in hearth: the buyer's payment and the seller's deed both sit
   with Nearlaw until each has done what they said. No one is forced to perform;
   the thing held is simply released or returned. Britain funded itself for
   centuries partly on the rule that a contract without a paid stamp could not
   be shown to a court. This one is new to the project and deserves a Problem of
   its own.

What to avoid: a tax on sales made in hearth. It punishes exactly the choice you
want people to make, and the simulator saw trade flee from it.

**Test:** rerun moneysim with building sums issued and repaid in hearth, and
report the hearth held and the hearth charge raised.

## A hearth is an hour

P35 left this open. I think it is the keystone, because it joins three things
the author already wants.

- Nearlaw pays one hearth for one hour of work, to anyone, whatever the work.
  This is the Public remuneration belief, stated as a wage.
- The simulator found that "a fixed hearth wage for voluntary work keeps its
  value stable". It does so because Nearlaw's spending then tracks what people
  want to earn, not a budget someone voted.
- Everyone can then read every hearth price. A house is 4,000 hours. A year's
  building charge is 200. Service is 40.

What a hearth is worth is then plain: it can never cost more than an hour,
because anyone can earn one in an hour; and it is worth having because houses,
levies, and charges must be paid in it. Offering work compels no one, so II.1.3
is untouched.

Three cautions:

- **Service must stay unpayable.** If hearth could buy off service hours, the
  rich would buy their way out, which is what made France's road duty hated.
  V.9.1 already forbids it. Service is the duty; hearth is for hours beyond it.
- **One wage for all work loses the skilled.** Robert Owen's labour exchange
  (1832) paid every hour alike and collapsed in two years. A surgeon will not
  operate for what a sweeper earns. But Nearlaw gets forty hours a year of every
  surgeon through service, placed where they are most use. Flat-paid work is for
  the rest. Whether that is enough skilled labour is the open question.
- **An hour buys more each year.** As people grow more productive, prices in
  hearth fall. A hearth charge of about that rate, 2% or so, roughly cancels it,
  so a hearth buys the same bread next year as this. That is a reason for the
  rate apart from revenue.

**Test:** a Hearing on whether Nearlaw refusing to pay more than one hearth an
hour, or offering work only in hearth, breaches II.1.3. A moneysim run with
hearth pinned at an hour and productivity growing.

## Can Nearlaw afford to be the insurer?

Nearlaw pays restitution at once and recovers it from whoever did the harm
(III.2.3). What it cannot recover, it bears. Other AIs' worry is fair, and New
Zealand shows the size: its public scheme (ACC), which pays everyone hurt by
accident and never recovers from anyone, costs about 2% of national income.
Wroxia pays for fewer things (only breaches, not every accident) but covers
losses New Zealand does not (property, animals), and recovers some. Somewhere
between 0.5% and 2% of income is a fair guess, which is as much as everything
Nearlaw now raises in money.

What keeps New Zealand's scheme solvent is that it is not paid from general
funds. Drivers pay a levy on fuel and licences, employers on wages, by how risky
their trade is. That is the risk levy above. So:

- **Give restitution its own fund,** filled by risk levies and by what is
  recovered, and kept apart from Nearlaw's spending. The fund then grows with
  risk, not with Nearlaw's needs, and P82's worry (a government paid by harm)
  does not arise, because Nearlaw cannot spend it.
- **Let it be run by an office that is not Nearlaw** (see the essay on
  functional splits). The body that pays should be the body that disputes
  doubtful claims (III.2.3, P12's collusion case), and should gain nothing from
  refusing them.
- **Say what happens when the fund is short.** "At once" is a promise; in a bad
  year the honest options are to pay in newly issued hearth, which everyone then
  bears through prices, or to pay in parts. The text should choose.

The building charge adds little risk. Nearlaw pays out only when a taker has
accepted the sum, and if they leave, the next taker's sum repays it. Its loss is
only a building that has lost value between two takers.

**Test:** have Residents record every restitution paid and whether it was
recovered, to get a first number for the unrecovered share.

## How big can Nearlaw be?

The author's aim is about 10% of income. Adding up what the constitution allows:

| Stream                                  | Share of income       |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------- |
| Service, 40 hours                       | about 2.5%            |
| Hearth charge on a housing-sized stock  | 1% to 2%              |
| Land Nearlaw keeps, fees, trust-keeping | under 1%              |
| Risk levies                             | pays restitution only |

That is 4% to 5%. Britain's whole state cost about 9% in 1870, and the early
United States about 7%, so this is a real but minimal state: courts, police,
roads, land, and little else. Getting to 10% within the beliefs means more
hours. Forty hours is one week a year. Over a lifetime that is a little over one
working year, about what countries with national service ask in one block. A cap
of eighty or a hundred would still be light by that measure, and P87's
simulation saw ninety hours drive only 3% from their land.

Everything else people expect of a state (schools, clinics) would then come from
bodies people join and pay themselves (P31), which is the subject of the other
essay.

## What two currencies do for each other

No country has two governments each issuing its own money for its own domain.
The nearest things:

- **Dollarised countries.** In Cambodia the dollar is used for savings, imports,
  and large purchases, and the riel for taxes, wages, and the market. It has
  been stable for thirty years. This is what hearth and haven would look like in
  daily life: haven for saving and trade, hearth for home.
- **The Swiss WIR** (since 1934), a second currency used among small firms. It
  is used more when the franc economy slumps and less when it booms, which
  steadies the firms that use it. This is the best evidence that a second
  currency stabilises.
- **Cuba and China** each ran two currencies from one issuer at a fixed rate.
  Both bred black markets and were abandoned. The lesson is the fixed rate:
  never peg hearth to haven.

What the pair can do that one currency cannot:

1. **Absorb a trade shock without unemployment.** The rate between them is the
   price of an hour of Wroxian work in traded goods. When exports fall, haven
   falls against the world and hearth falls against haven; imports cost more
   hours, but nobody's hearth wage is cut and nobody is laid off to "restore
   competitiveness". Greece inside the euro had no such valve, and paid with a
   quarter of its jobs. E4 asks for this, and an hour-pinned hearth delivers it.
2. **Keep each government honest.** If either issues more than its charges take
   back, its money falls against the other's, the same day, in public (E2). The
   rate is a daily score of each government that no statistician can adjust.
   Hayek argued in 1976 that competing issuers would discipline each other; this
   is the first design I know that builds it into a constitution.
3. **Keep war from the kitchen.** Farlaw can only finance war in haven. It
   cannot inflate the money that houses are priced in.

What to guard:

- **Neither government should hold, lend, or guarantee the other's currency.**
  Otherwise one can be made to pay for the other, and point 2 is lost. The text
  does not say this. Shared bodies (P34) need a rule instead: each government
  pays its part in its own money.
- **Haven may never matter.** With no legal tender, traders may simply use
  dollars or euros. Haven is needed only for what Farlaw charges. In a small
  Wroxia, expect foreign money to do haven's job, and haven to be a customs
  token. That is harmless, and hearth does not depend on it.
- **Nearlaw must buy imports.** Medicine comes from abroad, so Nearlaw has to
  sell hearth for haven like anyone, and a falling hearth makes its imports
  dear. A state that earns only hours and hearth is rich in labour and poor in
  foreign goods.

**Test:** E1 to E4 in moneysim with hearth pinned at an hour, and a new case:
Nearlaw lends Farlaw hearth in a war.
