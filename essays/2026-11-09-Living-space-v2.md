# Living space

A model of land for Wroxia, drawn out of rounds of quick questions on 9
November. Everyone gets an equal slice of the world to close to others or open
to them. No one can stack slices up, and no one needs a landlord.

## The aim

The wrong this model targets is dominion, not rent. In your words: "I have a
second house that this couple lives in; they haven't got a house of their own,
and so I have great dominion over them." Rent between two people who each have a
home is just association. Rent from someone with nowhere else to go is
domination.

Political philosophy calls this freedom as non-domination (Philip Pettit): you
are unfree if someone could interfere with you at will, even if they never do.
Your name for capitalism's pillar has a pedigree too: Thorstein Veblen's last
major book was _Absentee Ownership_ (1923).

The consequence is the model's central idea. **What protects a guest is the
exit, not tenant law.** A host may ask a guest to leave because the guest always
has a home of their own to go to. So Wroxia needs no law of tenancy at all.

## The model

1. **One quota.** Every adult has the same budget of points for land, whatever
   it is used for: home, workplace, or both. No one ever has to classify land.
   Points are not money and cannot be given to another person, only pooled.
2. **Paying.** Points are paid once, on taking up a parcel, at that day's price,
   which is set by demand. Points not spent stay free to use elsewhere. On
   leaving, you get back exactly what you paid: you keep a rising area's gain
   only by living there, and moving never wins or loses you points.
3. **Title, not presence.** What you hold is what your points are spent on, not
   where you happen to be. A patient in hospital, someone mid-move, or someone
   who just leaves the place empty keeps it. Leaving land empty is a right, and
   it still costs you the points.
4. **Guests and easements.** Anyone may host anyone on any agreed terms, money
   included, without the state knowing, and a guest stays at the host's
   pleasure. Anyone may also give others access to part of what they hold by
   contract, for any purpose, such as employees using a workshop at home; no
   official asks what an easement is for. The one limit: no one else may have
   exclusive use of all you hold for longer than "away for a time".
5. **A home at once.** Nearlaw keeps enough vacant homes, to a minimum standard
   and not shared, that any unspent quota can become a home immediately. It need
   not be where you would choose: a first home may be as makeshift as student
   digs, and you can stay with your parents until you find somewhere to plant
   shallow roots. This is the floor beneath everything else; without it, an
   18-year-old with a quota but no home is the couple in the second house.
6. **Parcels, not houses.** Beyond that reserve, Wroxia owes you land, not a
   building. When a parcel falls vacant:
   - **A named heir** may take it first, at a price capped at their whole quota.
     They can inherit a home worth more than a quota, but it then uses up every
     point they have.
   - **Otherwise it may be re-cut.** Within a short time limit, a body of
     neighbours chosen by lot may split it or merge it with others.
   - **A draw then halves it.** Each resulting parcel is drawn into one of two
     halves. A draw rather than strict alternation means no one can time a
     departure to land a parcel in the nominated half.
   - **The nominated half** goes to people the neighbours have named, such as
     the village boy away at university. A nomination can be revoked until the
     vacancy arises, and is void if anything of value was given for it. If no
     nominee takes the parcel within a time limit, it passes to lot.
   - **The lot half** is drawn first among those who listed the area as one of
     their three priority areas, which each person may change one at a time
     every few years, then among anyone.

   The neighbours cut before the draw, so they never know whether their own
   nominee or a stranger will get which piece. Strangers can always move in
   eventually, and a community can still renew itself.

7. **Buildings.** What you can move goes with you, so living in a caravan costs
   nothing on leaving. What you cannot move is valued by demand: it is worth
   however much the parcel's points price exceeds a bare parcel's, which Nearlaw
   pays you in money at a fixed rate. Leaving never costs you anything. A
   building nobody wants just makes the parcel cheaper for the next holder,
   warts and diamonds alike. Real harm, such as contamination, stays the
   leaver's liability.
8. **Firms and communes.** A firm or co-operative holds land only as points
   pooled from its members' quotas, so no company holds land in its own right.
   Communes start informally, at the host's pleasure; making one formal means
   pooling points, with shared title and easements easy to arrange. Formal
   pooling carries obligations that informal hosting does not. Communards can
   afford to leave their own land because doing so leaves them no worse off than
   anyone else.
9. **Children.** Children have no quota. A household gets a smaller allowance
   for each dependant, which ends when the child comes of age with their own.
10. **Resources.** A quota covers use of the surface. Extracting minerals,
    water, or anything else needs a licence, and its rent goes into a fund paid
    out equally to everyone, as Alaska's oil revenue is, never into a budget. No
    one, and no community, gains by extracting more, and the Negative Privileges
    Advocate may object for the animals and wild land affected.
11. **Revenue.** Nothing taxes labour or the equal quota, since a tax on equal
    holdings is a poll tax. Nearlaw instead draws on three sources:
    - **Unclaimed land, rented.** Land nobody claims with points may be rented
      for money a year at a time, never owned, and anyone with a quota may claim
      it, after notice, at the next vacancy. Those who have more pay more, and
      land cannot hide from the register.
    - **Pollution charges,** set at the harm done by a body independent of
      Nearlaw, such as farlaw or the Advocate, so Nearlaw gains nothing by
      letting pollution build up.
    - **A fixed amount per person, taken from the resource dividend** before it
      is paid out. It is a poll tax in form, but nobody pays it from their own
      pocket.

## Distortions you accept

- **Incumbents keep windfalls while they stay.** Whoever lives in a place that
  becomes popular has it better than later arrivals, and can draw income from
  that through guests or by letting while away. Leaving costs them the
  advantage.
- **Better, rarely more.** A family can keep a grand house through generations,
  but each heir spends their whole quota on it. Money buys more only of land
  nobody else wants, and never for keeps.
- **Strangers wait.** In a desirable area, strangers get half of what falls
  vacant, by lot.
- **Guests have no security.** This is acceptable only because a home is always
  available.
- **First homes may be far from where you would choose.**
- **Families with children have less space per head** than households of adults.
- **One large state machine:** the reserve of homes, payment for buildings, and
  the rounds that set prices.

## What it would change in Wroxia

- **I.2.3** would make title rest on the quota rather than on occupancy, which
  would remain the fact of living or working somewhere. That separates the two
  jobs occupancy now does, and answers the hospital patient, the door in the
  empty building (P56), the gap mid-move (P64), and key money (P63), since
  neither land nor nominations can be sold.
- **P61** would be answered: crofters protect their common grazing by pooling
  points into it, so a newcomer cannot fence a plot on it.
- **P25** would close: renting unclaimed land replaces land value tax and its
  allowance. P58's problem of collecting land tax becomes collecting rent from a
  register, though prices in points still have to be set.
- **P62's underinvestment** eases: an orchard planted on land you hold stays
  yours while you hold it, and is paid for when you leave.
- **P39** would be answered for land, but not for money or things.
- **Q6's floor** would be the home at once, and **Q8** would matter less, since
  no one need live in a park.
- The quota would be the first substantial positive privilege (II.8). The
  Constitution itself needs only an equal quota that cannot be given away, a
  home at once, and no holding land except through quotas. Vacancies and ties
  would be an instrument; prices, the reserve, and the rate for buildings would
  be nearlaw.

## Still open

- **Who has a quota:** citizens, residents, or every patient? Wild land and the
  animals living on it might need a share held for them by the Advocate. And if
  citizenship gives a quota, existing citizens gain from admitting fewer people.
- **Prices:** how demand sets them in practice, perhaps by periodic rounds in
  which everyone bids with equal budgets, as in Hylland and Zeckhauser's
  pseudo-markets. The same prices value buildings, so the money rate for them
  must not become a political lever.
- **A guest who won't leave:** the one moment the state must know about hosting.
  Removal needs a rule, made easy by the home at once.
- **Separation:** who keeps a pooled home when a couple or co-operative splits,
  especially where there is abuse.
- **Leaving a firm or co-operative** with your points, without leaving it
  stranded.
- **How long "away for a time"** may last before letting all you hold becomes
  landlordism.
- **Emigrating:** whether a quota lapses on leaving Wroxia; otherwise you are an
  absentee owner abroad.
- **Enough revenue:** unclaimed land may be scarce and pollution should fall, so
  the dividend may have to carry most of the burden. If the poll tax ever
  exceeds the dividend, people would pay it from their own pockets.
- **Children's guardianship** generally, beyond space.

## Next

Hold the model against one test: does it still leave anyone feeling the dread of
a mortgage or a landlord? Then, if you're comfortable with it, it becomes one
Problem, a few Questions, and hearing cases: a lodger asked to leave, an heir to
a farm worth more than a quota, two nominees for one cottage, an 18-year-old
claiming a home at once, crofters pooling their common grazing, and the builder
of a large house moving away.
